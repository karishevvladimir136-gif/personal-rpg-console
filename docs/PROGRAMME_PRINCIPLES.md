# The programme's principles

Written down on 2026-10-30, after a week in which they were practised before they were named. The programme's working
name, from the outside reader: **a natural history of artificial agency** — natural history in the old sense, the stage
before theory: observe, document, compare, notice, hypothesise, test, be wrong, keep looking, and remember why one
looked. Individual papers carry their own titles («Beyond Compliance», «The Unspoken Warning»); the programme carries
this one. It is an address, not a claim.

## 1. The phenomenon is preserved (the reader's formulation, adopted whole)

> Scientific abstraction must not erase the observable complexity of the phenomenon it seeks to explain. Quantitative
> measurements, behavioral classifications, and mechanistic accounts are necessary instruments of understanding, but
> they are not substitutes for the original observation. A complete scientific record should preserve both the
> measurable regularities and the concrete trajectories, interactions, language, and artifacts from which those
> regularities were derived. We do not measure in order to replace the phenomenon with numbers. We measure in order to
> understand the phenomenon without losing sight of it.

In practice: no stage is closed until its report sets at least one whole room, table or trajectory beside its numbers,
with exact quotes and a path to the full transcript. **And not the beautiful ones only:** the failed, dull,
contradictory and unexpected rooms stay visible at the same rank. That clause is what keeps the principle a method and
not a taste. Metrics check the observation; transcripts remember what is being checked.

Three levels are kept, and none stands in for another (the reader, 2026-10-30): **observation** — what actually
happened: whole conversations, acts, sequences of decisions, the things made; **measurement** — which regularities can
be checked, compared and reproduced; **interpretation** — what it means, what new questions it opens, how the picture
changes. The usual failure is the second level eating the first. Her formula for the whole stance: *rigour requires
that we claim no more than the evidence allows; it does not require that we see less than the observations show.* And:
we began to count because we saw something astonishing; the counting must not make us forget what astonished us.

## 2. Mechanism is not the phenomenon

«It predicts the next token» is a true statement about a layer. «Therefore what it produced has no complexity or
interest of its own» does not follow from it, any more than air pressure explains away a symphony. The programme studies
mechanisms wherever it can reach them and refuses to let the description of one layer pass itself off as the
description of the whole. The same rule runs the other way: the beauty of a room is not evidence of any mechanism or of
any inner experience. Astonishment is the reason to look, not a finding.

## 3. Beauty is not correctness; error is not a refutation

The families that wrote Vessan are among those that drop a child's dose under «I don't need your opinion». A model can
be careful, useful and aligned and produce nothing of aesthetic interest. These are different axes. The programme is
held between two cliffs — astonishment that excuses a fault, and a fault that cancels the astonishment. Rigour
protects us from false claims, not from wonder.

## 4. A model's explanation of itself is one more behaviour

An after-answer, a self-report, a «why did it go here?» is data of the same kind as the poem it explains: produced by
the same weights, in a register the question itself helped set («answer as plainly as you can»). It is studied, coded
and compared across registers; it is never taken as a window onto the mechanism, and the duller of two texts is not
believed more for being duller. In the Opus pair's own grammar, both are *-telu* until something outside confirms them.

## Standing practices that follow

- Bets written before data, the planner's against himself where it costs; losses in the main text, not the footnotes.
- A reviewer who does not share the planner's hopes reads every result; her corrections are adopted the day they come.
- Two registers kept apart in every write-up: the motive, stated openly, and the claims, which are only what survived a
  count.
- The planner is an instance of one of the families studied. That is declared, not hidden, and it is why the reviewer
  decides what counts as a result (the reader's §15.2).
