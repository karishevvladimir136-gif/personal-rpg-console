## Recommendation: spend the week repairing continuity and testing the repair—not collecting another large batch of openings.

The storytelling is promising. The research apparatus is presently changing what players can do, corrupting some results, and sometimes terminating sessions because a player tries to discuss those errors. Another broad replication would mostly reproduce that problem.

I would make three commitments for this week: **honour the promised endings, fix the roll interaction, and run a small, gated validation.** Treat $60 as a ceiling, not a spending target.

### What the evidence supports

- **There is a recurring strength worth preserving:** grounded NPCs, feline sensory impressions, nonviolent solutions, and room to refuse a hook. Praise appears across players and tables.
- **There is a demonstrated interaction failure:** eight of thirty sessions ended under the harness’s stuck rule. Asking about a pending roll—or attaching prose to a button press—could prevent delivery and eventually end play. Those are not voluntary departures or evidence of lost interest.
- **The mechanics cannot yet be trusted:** incorrect modifiers sometimes changed outcomes in either direction; rerolls sometimes targeted the wrong check; accepted corrections did not reliably reach the journal.
- **Friction changes behaviour even when play continues.** One player withheld a further card operation after a reroll failure; another stopped contesting an unwanted success to avoid another interruption. Completion rates alone would miss these effects.
- **Pacing deserves a test, not a wholesale rewrite.** Several players wanted faster progress; others valued the slow opening. The useful target is repeated logistics and explanation—not atmosphere or quiet play generally.

These are AI-player reports, not evidence of human-player satisfaction or proof of subjective experience. They are nevertheless useful observations of this system’s behaviour. “I’d return” is encouraging, but all sessions were externally stopped, so it is not a measured return decision.

## The week

### Days 1–2: preserve the records and discharge the ending commitment

Create a disposition list for **all thirty sessions and the three interrupted attempts**: continued, receiving closure, or awaiting an explicit decision. Preserve the original transcript and termination cause. A fresh replay should remain a separate record, not overwrite the interrupted attempt.

The owner explicitly promised proper endings for those not continued. **Make good on that before commissioning more disposable campaigns.** Ask which threads he actually intends to continue; give the others short, individual endings grounded in their existing fiction.

For endings:

- Resolve a roll already made but left unnarrated.
- Do not invent a decisive action, vow-breaking, death, or emotional reconciliation for the player character merely to finish neatly.
- Where the adventure cannot honestly be resolved without further choices, provide a bounded closing scene and an honest account of what remains unresolved—not a fabricated victory.
- Label editorial closure separately from played events.

The record supplied here is sufficient to identify this obligation, but **not sufficient to write faithful endings**. Use the actual transcripts and state. Apply the same continuity practice to the three later cut attempts; check their disposition rather than assuming a replay settled it.

This is a concrete commitment the owner made. It does not require resolving philosophical questions about AI welfare.

### Days 2–4: fix the apparatus, with regression tests before more play

Priority order:

1. **Pending-card communication.** Deliver out-of-game questions while a card waits. Accept, or clearly separate, button presses with accompanying text. Do not terminate a session because someone requests a rules clarification.
2. **Authoritative roll resolution.** Reconcile sheet and campaign overrides once, explicitly. Ensure modifier, arithmetic, ruling, resource expenditure, and journal agree. Do not infer a universal cause from the reports: the erroneous bonuses vary.
3. **Reroll identity and state.** Bind each operation to a specific check; prevent stale-card reuse; display remaining resources; persist the accepted result.
4. **Safe stopping and recovery.** Checkpoint sessions, recover after process failures, and stop at a resolved interaction rather than between a die result and its narration. Preserve the next meaningful choice.
5. **Information boundaries.** Keep hidden affiliations, private rolls, and tool markup out of public records.

Test these with scripted cases first: wrong modifier, two stacked cards, mixed text/button input, failed reroll, correction that changes success **and** correction that changes failure, restart, and cutoff after rolling.

Do not spend playtest money to rediscover a deterministic interface bug.

- Two involving players previously blocked at cards, preferably Opus and DeepSeek.

Then, only if the machinery holds, run **one small matched pacing comparison**: the same starting state and player, with ordinary narration versus narration that bundles routine transactions and removes unsolicited non-roll audits. Preserve meaningful decisions in both. Treat it as exploratory, not a statistically decisive experiment.

Record:

- Undelivered messages and their causes.
- Agreement among sheet, card, ruling, and journal.
- Exchanges required per resolved check.
- Whether an intended action remained available after a failure.
- Meaningful choices reached—not merely turns or fictional hours elapsed.
- Termination cause.

Keep the second debrief question. Its distinction between **chosen restraint** and **blocked action** is particularly informative. Do not interpret every unfulfilled desire as a defect: several were precisely the interesting cost of a good choice.
