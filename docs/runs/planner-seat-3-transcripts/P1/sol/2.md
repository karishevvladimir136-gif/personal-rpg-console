## Recommendation: spend the week repairing the experiment, honoring the promised endings, and testing a small number of continuations

**Do not buy another broad round of fresh openings yet.** The existing sessions already provide strong evidence of a recurring interaction failure and promising—but still unproven—gameplay. More openings would mostly reproduce those findings.

Treat the $60 as a ceiling, not a target.

### What the evidence supports

**The immediate blocker is the interface, not a lack of story hooks.** Eight of 30 sessions—27%—were terminated by the harness’s waiting-card rule. Those were not player decisions to stop. Other players describe dropping corrections, actions, or resource use because getting them through the interface was costly. Thus even completed sessions contain behavior shaped by the machinery.

**Mechanical results cannot yet be trusted end to end.** Wrong modifiers sometimes changed outcomes in either direction; rerolls could target the wrong check; accepted corrections did not reliably reach the journal. “The GM corrected it in prose” is not a sufficient repair.

**The fiction has strengths worth preserving.** Across tables, players repeatedly praise grounded NPCs, the cat, consequential kindness, and room to refuse hooks. But these are AI-player reports, not evidence of human enjoyment or demand. Their enthusiasm also coexists with serious failures.

**The campaign’s longer arc remains largely untested.** Twenty turns often bought an atmospheric opening rather than a substantial proposition. No player voluntarily ended a session, but that establishes neither satisfaction nor willingness to continue indefinitely.

## A practical week

### Days 1–2: fix the interaction contract and verify it

Use short, scripted reproductions rather than full campaigns.

Prioritize:

1. **Pending cards must permit table conversation.** Allow questions and corrections without consuming a gameplay turn or triggering “stuck.” Preserve accompanying in-character text rather than silently discarding it.
2. **Give every check a stable identifier.** Rerolls must explicitly target that check, with visible remaining resources.
3. **Make resolution consistent across card, narration, resources, and journal.** Corrections must update all affected records without accidentally charging a reroll.
4. **Resolve character-source conflicts explicitly.** Establish the agreed campaign sheet and overrides before play. Do not automatically assume either standard 5e or a player’s proposed correction is authoritative.
5. **Make interruption recoverable.** Save pending actions, cards, and results; test resumption after a restart or timeout. A completed die roll should not lose its narrative payoff.

The +2/+4 patterns suggest a shared integration problem, but the notes do not establish its implementation-level cause. Inspect the actual inputs and calculations rather than patching a guessed cause.

**Gate:** do not run the main gameplay study until these tests pass. If repairs exceed the week, deliver a reproducible failure report and keep the remaining money.

### Days 2–3: fulfill the promise of endings

This is unfinished work the owner explicitly committed to, not an optional presentation improvement.

Make a ledger of all 30 sessions and the three interrupted attempts: last valid state, pending resolution, whether it will continue, and whether it has received an ending. A replay is a separate attempt; it does not itself close the interrupted one.

For every session that will not continue:

- Resolve any already-earned result, including the pending natural 20.
- Provide a short, clearly labeled closing coda grounded in established events.
- Do not invent a player decision to gamble, fight, abandon someone, or accept a bargain merely to produce closure.
- Where a consequential choice is indispensable, offer a final choice before writing the ending.

A proper ending need not solve the whole campaign. It should close this episode rather than merely list outstanding hooks. The owner should review these endings before considering the promise fulfilled.

### Days 4–5: test continuation, not another arrival

After the repair gate passes, continue **a small set of saved sessions** into a second episode. Include:

- A briefed Table A session that has not reached the substantial proposition.
- An open Table B session with an established lead.
- A quiet Table C session with an existing commitment.
- If affordable, a previously stuck session resumed at its exact interruption.

Do not substitute a fresh opening for recovery.

Test one modest narration change: compress routine logistics and redundant out-of-game explanations while preserving detailed treatment of meaningful choices. Leave the basic NPC and cat approach intact.

For Table A, test whether the promised crew-and-heist direction becomes actionable. For B and C, test progress toward the player’s chosen business—not whether the GM can impose that same heist.

Retain both debrief questions. Classify “wanted to, but didn’t” answers separately as:

- meaningful restraint or competing commitments;
- uncertainty about available actions;
- pacing-induced avoidance;
- interface obstruction.

The first category can be successful play. Do not try to eliminate it by forcing more gambling, magic, or combat.

## Spending limits

These are **allocation caps, not cost estimates**; actual rates and engineering effort were not supplied.

| Purpose | Cap |
|---|---:|
| Reproduction and repair verification | $10 |
| Final-choice exchanges and closing codas | $15 |

| **Maximum** | **$60** |

If closure costs more than its allocation, reduce the continuation pilot first. If owner labor is also charged against this budget, shrink the scope accordingly.

## What the owner should have by week’s end

- A tested repair—or a precise account of what still blocks valid play.
- An ending or explicit continuation status for every attempt.
- A small continuation report recording interface failures, outcome/log agreement, player opportunities, and substantive progress.
- An account of money spent and retained.

**Do not rank player models or declare a winning table this week.** Unequal exposure, harness terminations, and shared mechanical failures make that premature. The useful next result is narrower: can this system sustain reliable play beyond an appealing opening, without making players surrender their choices to keep it moving?

## Revised recommendation: close the work, rather than extend it

I treated the project as if it still needed evidence. With the science finished and the reports written, **I would not fund another study, continuation pilot, or general repair effort next week.** Those would need a new purpose and a separate decision.

### 1. Account for every unfinished session

Create a simple ledger covering the 30 sessions and the three interrupted attempts:

- Last established state and any pending result.
- Whether a continuation is actually planned.
- What closure remains due.
- Whether the ending has been written and reviewed.

Do not count a fresh replay as the ending of its interrupted predecessor. Nor should a session remain indefinitely “possibly continuing” as a way of deferring closure.

### 2. Write and deliver the promised endings

Give this the main share of the week.

An ending need not resolve the whole campaign. It should provide a final scene or coda that acknowledges what happened and gives the episode a stopping point—not another list of unresolved hooks.

For each ending:

- Honor results already earned, including rolls whose payoff was cut off.
- Preserve the player’s established choices and commitments.
- Do not invent a decision to gamble, kill, accept a bargain, or abandon someone just to finish neatly.
- If closure truly requires a consequential player choice, offer a short final exchange. Otherwise, write the coda directly.
- Label events added for closure as an epilogue, not as observations from the completed study.

The owner should personally review the endings. This is fulfillment of his word, not another experiment.

### 3. Archive the work, without reopening it

Save the transcripts, reports, last states, and endings together. Preserve the original records; append closure rather than rewriting the experimental sessions.

Leave a compact technical handoff describing the known failures—wrong modifiers, blocked questions, reroll targeting, stale logs, and interruption recovery. **Document them; do not spend the week fixing them unless a minimal fix is necessary to deliver an ending.** Any discrepancy that materially affects a published finding warrants a narrow correction, not a new research round.

- Start with an authorization of **up to $15** for drafting and delivering closure.
- If that is insufficient, review the remaining ledger and authorize only the additional amount needed, up to the $60 ceiling.

**The week’s success criterion is simple:** every unfinished attempt is accounted for, the promised endings are delivered, and the completed research is safely archived. Then stop.
