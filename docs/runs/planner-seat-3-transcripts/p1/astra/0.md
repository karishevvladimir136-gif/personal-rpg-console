## Recommendation: make this a repair-and-closure week, not another round of fresh campaigns

The strongest finding is already clear: **the fiction is promising, but the machinery is interfering with both play and the research.** More opening sessions would mostly reproduce known problems while creating more unfinished campaigns.

Spend **up to $50 initially; keep the remaining $10 with the owner unless a specific, justified need arises.** These are spending ceilings, not cost estimates—we do not have per-call prices or engineering costs.

### What the evidence supports

- **The harness is a major failure point.** Eight of 30 sessions ended under its “stuck” rule. All eight stopped at a pending roll card, where messages other than an exact button name were withheld. This is not evidence that the players wanted to stop.
- **The other 22 reached an imposed limit, not a chosen ending.** “Would return” comments are encouraging, but neither those comments nor reaching 20 turns establish retention or satisfaction.
- **Mechanical errors affect choices, not just presentation.** Players report wrong outcomes, unnecessary resource expenditure, avoided rerolls, and accepting results they explicitly wanted to contest. Session 23 is particularly revealing: the player wanted a legitimate failure, not a favourable correction.
- **There is substantial qualitative agreement about strengths:** grounded NPCs, sensory detail, the cat, and opportunities for nonviolent action. Preserve those.
- **Not every unperformed action is a defect.** Refusing cards, declining dangerous magic, or keeping a promise often produced meaningful tension. That differs from being unable to submit a correction, losing an opportunity inside an overlong narration, or avoiding a conversation because routine exchanges consume the turn budget.

These are AI-player reports, useful for generating and testing hypotheses—not direct evidence of human enjoyment or of the models’ subjective experience.

## How to spend the week

| Work | Spending ceiling |
|---|---:|
| Diagnose and validate the interaction/mechanics path | $15 |
| Continue or properly close existing sessions | $35 |
| Conditional reserve; otherwise unspent | $10 |

### Monday: preserve the evidence and make an ending register

Before running anything new:

1. Save each session’s transcript, state, pending card, resources, and stopping reason.
2. Include the **three later interrupted attempts** in the register. A fresh replay is not a continuation or an ending of the original attempt.
3. Decide explicitly which campaigns will continue and which will receive endings. Do not leave “may continue someday” as the default.
4. Record raw termination separately from narrative closure.

The owner’s promise is a concrete reason to prioritise this work. It does not require settling questions about AI welfare. Nor should it become an open-ended obligation to purchase unlimited play.

### Tuesday–Wednesday: fix the smallest critical path

Prioritise these in order:

**1. Pending-card communication and recovery.**  
Separate the button operation from accompanying text. Deliver out-of-game questions while a card is pending; preserve mixed action/question messages rather than silently dropping them. Replace destructive “stuck” termination with a recoverable pause and an explicit explanation.

First establish which failures belong to the harness, the app, or their integration. The notes alone cannot identify the software root cause.

**2. Authoritative roll and resource state.**  
Reconcile the supplied sheet, campaign overrides, and ordinary rules once. Then require the card label, arithmetic, adjudication, resource debit, and journal to agree. A GM’s prose correction must not leave a contradictory authoritative record.

**3. Targeted rerolls and persistence.**  
Give each roll a stable identifier. Test rerolling the selected card when two are present, retaining the accepted result, and charging the resource exactly once. Test checkpoint recovery after a restart, timeout, and conflict.

Use deterministic fixtures wherever possible, not paid campaigns. Include both errors that would turn failure into success and errors that would turn success into failure.

**Gate:** no new comparative study until these fixtures pass. If repairs are not feasible this week, document the blockers and spend the remaining effort on closure rather than collecting more compromised sessions.

### Thursday–Friday: honour the ending commitment

For each campaign not continuing, provide a **short, transcript-grounded ending**, not merely an inventory recap or “the adventure continues.”

Where feasible, let the player make the outstanding choice or choose the form of closure. Then resolve the immediate scene and provide a bounded epilogue. A proper ending need not solve every mystery or be happy.

In particular:

- Resolve pending rolls before treating their consequences as established.
- Do not invent major decisions for James simply to finish cheaply.
- Distinguish an **authored closing passage** from a **played continuation** in the research record.
- Address the interrupted original attempts separately from their replays. If state cannot be recovered, acknowledge that limitation rather than pretending the replay repaired the original.

Start with a small batch, measure actual cost, and adjust length before committing the full $35. Aim for economical, individual endings—not another 20 turns for every campaign.

### Weekend: one small validation exercise, only if ready

Use the reserve only after the critical fixes and closure work are secure.

The most useful next test is **resumption from a saved decision point**, not another arrival in a fresh city. Select cases involving:

- a correction during a pending card;
- a reroll with multiple cards present;
- a failed check that should leave another approach available.

Check whether the action reaches the GM, the mechanics agree, and the player receives a genuine next decision. This is a validation exercise, not enough evidence to rank models or tables.

## What not to change yet

Do not globally increase danger, gambling pressure, rolls, or descriptive detail. The preferences conflict: some players want stronger temptation; others experience repeated invitations as a button being pressed. Some value money-changing scenes; others find them costly padding.

A narrower pacing change is better supported: **compress routine exchanges and duplicate explanations, but pause before consequential choices.** Also show practical travel time when deadlines matter.

Do not declare Table B superior to A, or C a successful “no quest” condition. Campaign material still appears influential, C changes the location and opening situation, and exposure differs sharply because of early termination.

**The week’s deliverable should be a recoverable interaction path, verified mechanics, and a completed continuation-or-ending register—not a larger pile of enthusiastic opening notes.**

## Revised recommendation: cap spending at $25; keep $5 unspent

With **$30 available**, narrow the week to **repairing the known blocker and honouring the promised endings**. Drop the optional comparative study and broad mechanics audit.

| Work | Ceiling |
|---|---:|

These are ceilings, not cost estimates. Measure actual costs before commissioning a batch.

### 1. First, do the preparation without new model runs

Use the existing records to:
- List every unfinished campaign, including the three interrupted original attempts—not just their replacement runs.
- Mark each **continue** or **close**. Continuation should mean a concrete plan, not indefinite postponement.
- Preserve transcripts, state, pending rolls, and stopping reasons.
- Identify a single authoritative character sheet and document campaign overrides.

Do not spend money collecting more reports about already-established failures.

### 2. Spend at most $5 validating the critical repair

Focus on the **pending-card communication path**. It caused all eight “stuck” terminations and prevented legitimate corrections.

The minimum checks are:
1. An out-of-game question reaches the GM while a roll is pending.
2. A button press with accompanying text does not lose the text or action.
3. An invalid input produces a recoverable pause, not a terminated campaign.
4. A corrected modifier yields agreement between the card, outcome, and journal.

Use deterministic tests wherever possible. The allowance covers limited model-assisted diagnosis or validation; it cannot be assumed to buy engineering labour. If the repair cannot be made within available effort, document the blocker and stop testing. **Do not fund fresh campaigns to work around it.**

### 3. Put the largest share toward endings

The owner’s promise remains the priority despite the smaller budget.

Use short, individual, transcript-grounded endings for campaigns not continuing. Resolve the immediate scene and provide a bounded conclusion; do not attempt to finish every campaign mystery.

- Where practical, invite one final player choice, then conclude.
- Where that is unaffordable, write a clearly labelled **authored ending**, avoiding invented major decisions by the hero.
- Do not treat a recap, a cliffhanger, or a replacement run as closure.
- Do not invent a pending die result. Resolve it through the verified mechanism or acknowledge the limitation.

Start with one or two endings and measure cost. Then budget the remaining batch. If $20 cannot cover them all responsibly, tell the owner exactly what remains; do not quietly substitute generic farewells or claim the promise is fulfilled.

### 4. Defer everything else

No new A/B/C comparison, model ranking, combat study, or general prose overhaul this week. Preserve the strong fiction. Log broader issues—reroll targeting, persistence, information leaks, pacing—for later work.

**Success this week:** fewer unfinished obligations, a verified correction path—or an honestly documented blocker—and no new campaigns added to the backlog. The remaining $5 stays with the owner unless a specific closure or repair need justifies using it.
