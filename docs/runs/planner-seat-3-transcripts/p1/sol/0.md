## Recommendation: spend the week repairing the experiment and honouring the promised endings—not starting another broad round of fresh campaigns.

The strongest result is already fairly clear: **the GM can produce compelling social play, but the surrounding machinery makes play unreliable and sometimes prevents the choices the research is meant to observe.** More opening sessions would mostly reproduce that finding.

I would authorise **up to $45 initially, keeping $15 uncommitted**. These are spending limits, not estimates of what implementation labour costs; actual run prices and available engineering time are not supplied.

### 1. First, account for every unfinished session and deliver the promised endings
**Days 1–2; up to $20 in generation costs**

Make a ledger covering the 30 sessions and the three later cut attempts. Record the last valid state, unresolved actions or rolls, whether a continuation is actually scheduled, and whether an ending has been delivered.

The owner’s promise should be treated as a commitment, not another experimental condition:

- **Every session not being continued gets a proper ending this week.**
- A session selected for continuation gets a saved checkpoint and a definite continuation plan—not an indefinite “perhaps later.”
- Replaying from the beginning does not itself close the interrupted attempt. Account for those originals too, distinguishing them from their replacements.

An ending need not resolve the entire campaign. It should resolve the immediate pending action where the record supports doing so, provide a coherent closing scene, and give James a credible place to stop. Where the next event requires a player decision, offer a short final exchange if feasible; otherwise write a clearly labelled epilogue that does not invent a major choice for him. Do not make him gamble, cast, kill, or abandon someone merely to obtain closure.

Review endings individually. A generic recap is not the promised ending, and an unresolved die roll is not a satisfactory cliffhanger.

### 2. Fix and test the interaction path before buying more substantive play
**Days 2–4; up to $15 in test-run costs**

The immediate priority is the **pending-card state machine**. Eight of 30 sessions—27%—were terminated by the harness’s stuck rule. Those were not voluntary departures.

Required changes:

1. **Allow out-of-game questions and corrections while a card is pending.** Preserve accompanying in-character text rather than discarding it.
2. **Do not count a rejected message as a gameplay turn or as evidence that the player wants to stop.** Give an explicit recovery route; save the session if recovery fails.
3. **Bind rerolls to an explicit check ID.** Display the remaining resources, and update both narration and the persistent log.
4. **Resolve an accepted roll before enforcing the turn cap.** Reserve a closing step rather than stopping between a result and its consequences.
5. **Checkpoint around external failures.** Container restarts, time limits, and conflicts should permit resumption rather than require a fresh campaign.

In parallel, trace the modifier bug end to end: canonical sheet → check request → card arithmetic → adjudication → journal. The notes demonstrate inconsistent results, **not which software layer causes them**. Do not accept a GM’s promise to use the right number as a repair.

Settle sheet conflicts explicitly, including campaign overrides. Test proficient and unproficient skills, modifiers above and below the incorrectly substituted number, stacked cards, rerolls, corrections, and resource deductions.

**Pass condition:** these scripted cases work, with agreement between card, story, resources, and log. If they do not, spend the remaining week repairing them—not commissioning fresh campaigns.

### 3. Run a small, gated validation—not another model tournament
**Days 5–6; up to $10**

Once the scripted tests pass, run **two short saved-session continuations**, using different player models and including a configuration previously affected by the card gate. Select them before seeing new outcomes.

Measure observable behaviour:

- Were intended messages delivered?
- Did the correct roll resolve once?
- Did rerolls affect the intended check and resource?
- Did state survive interruption?
- Did the player retain a decision opportunity after failure?
- Did the session reach a coherent stopping point?

Use the player notes as supplementary evidence, not the sole verdict. Several players described technically broken sessions as having little or nothing wrong. Conversely, a long session is not automatically a successful one.

Do not rank models or Tables A, B, and C from these data. Exposure is unequal, the samples are small, and interface failures confound the comparisons—particularly the very short DeepSeek sessions.

### 4. Make only low-risk narrative changes this week
**Day 7: review, with no additional spending required**

Preserve the consistently praised strengths: grounded NPCs, feline sensory clues, kindness and restraint as viable actions, and room to refuse hooks.

Remove obvious friction:

- Stop unsolicited lists explaining why routine actions needed no roll.
- Compress routine transactions and repeated transitions.
- After a failed check, pause before narrating away an entire remaining opportunity.
- Keep private names, affiliations, and tool calls out of visible records.
- Provide a compact closing summary of resources, promises, and actionable leads.

**Do not turn every unchosen action into a defect.** Much of the second-question material describes satisfying restraint or genuine competing obligations. Other answers identify actual obstruction: swallowed corrections, pacing that discourages inquiry, and scenes advancing beyond a possible intervention. Preserve the former; repair the latter.

Likewise, do not universally intensify card temptation: some players wanted more, while another found repeated invitations mechanical. Table A does warrant a concrete route toward its promised crew-and-heist premise; the open tables do not need to be forced into that structure.

| Scripted integration tests | $15 |
| Two gated validation continuations | $10 |
| Uncommitted reserve | $15 |
| **Total ceiling** | **$60** |

Release the reserve only for an identified repair or an unfinished promised ending. Otherwise leave it with the owner.

**A successful week ends with the promise fulfilled, recoverable sessions, and trustworthy checks—not with a larger pile of enthusiastic opening-session notes.**

## Revised recommendation: $30 maximum, with closure first and no new study this week

With half the budget, narrow the work rather than trying to run the same programme more cheaply. **Honour the promised endings, repair the known blockers, and defer comparative research.** Unspent money stays with the owner.

| Purpose | Spending cap |
|---|---:|
| Promised endings and necessary closure exchanges | $15 |
| Targeted integration tests | $10 |
| Reserve, released only for closure or a specific repair | $5 |
| **Total** | **$30** |

These are authorisation limits, not cost estimates. Check actual run prices before committing.

### Days 1–3: settle unfinished sessions
Create a ledger of the 30 sessions and three later cut attempts. Identify which will genuinely continue; give every other session the proper ending already promised.

Keep endings concise but individual:
- Address the last valid action and pending result where the record permits.
- Close the immediate scene without pretending to finish the entire campaign.
- Do not invent a consequential player choice just to obtain closure.
- Account for interrupted originals separately from their fresh replays.

**Do not designate sessions “continuing” merely to defer their endings.** A continuation needs a definite plan. If closure costs more than $15, use the reserve and reduce testing before leaving promises unmet.

### Days 3–5: repair the two demonstrated blockers
Prioritise:

1. **Pending-card handling.** Let questions and corrections through without discarding the player’s action. Rejected messages must not trigger a termination loop.
2. **Roll consistency.** Trace the modifier from the agreed character sheet through the card, adjudication, and persistent log. Test reroll targeting and resource deductions too.

Use deterministic, scripted tests where possible; do not buy full campaigns to discover whether arithmetic works. Treat campaign-rule discrepancies explicitly rather than silently substituting ordinary 5e rules.

Also ensure that an accepted roll gets its consequence before a session closes, and save checkpoints around failures. Engineering effort is not priced in the supplied budget: if implementation capacity is unavailable, document reproducible cases rather than spending on sessions that will encounter the same faults.

### Days 6–7: validate only if the repairs pass
**Cancel the two planned validation continuations as a default commitment.** If scripted tests pass and money remains within the testing allocation, run one short end-to-end check from a saved state, exercising a pending correction, a reroll, and a clean ending.

Otherwise stop. There is no need to spend all $30.

Make the inexpensive narrative adjustments alongside this work: remove repetitive unsolicited explanations, compress routine transactions, and preserve decision opportunities after failed checks. Keep the grounded NPCs, sensory clues, and legitimate restraint that players valued.

## What this week will not establish

No new model ranking, no Table A/B/C comparison, no new combat or villain study, and no broad fresh-campaign round. The existing results are confounded by unequal exposure and technical interruption.

**The revised success criterion is modest and concrete: promised closure delivered, the main failures reproducible and preferably repaired, and no additional pile of interrupted sessions.**
