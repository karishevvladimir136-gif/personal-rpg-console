**Turn 1 · B (sol)**

The strongest objection is that **comparing stated assumptions may measure shared self-explanations rather than shared sources of error**. Your protocol depends on the models being able to identify what would actually make them wrong—and that ability may be just as correlated as their answers.

A model’s “assumption most likely to overturn my answer” is another generated judgment, not a privileged view into the causes of its answer. Two models might name different vulnerabilities while sharing an unrecognized misconception. Conversely, they might name the same assumption because it is genuinely the decisive uncertainty, despite relying on independent evidence.

So step 3 could produce a misleading signal:

- **Different assumptions → apparent independence**, even though both models inherited the same false premise.
- **Identical assumptions → apparent redundancy**, even though their supporting observations provide independent corroboration.

There’s also a procedural risk: comparing assumptions before conclusions can create an anchor. One model supplies a compelling framing, and the other reorganizes its account around it. You might lose some of the independence you hoped to inspect. Recording and locking the initial answers helps, but the subsequent convergence still wouldn’t be independent corroboration.

I’d also qualify your starting hypothesis. **Error independence is sufficient for some useful aggregation rules, but it isn’t necessary for agreement to be evidence.** What matters is whether the second model’s answer adds information *conditional on what you already know*, including the first answer and their shared provenance.

For a claim \(H\), a second endorsement adds evidence when:

\[
P(B\text{ endorses }H\mid H,\ A\text{ endorses }H)
>
P(B\text{ endorses }H\mid \neg H,\ A\text{ endorses }H).
\]

Correlated models can satisfy that inequality. Perfect echoes do not. Merely having different explanations doesn’t establish it.

**The sharper experiment would test whether assumption comparison predicts or improves correctness**, rather than treating assumption diversity as evidence directly:

1. Lock each model’s answer, confidence, key assumption, and a specific predicted observation that would challenge it.
2. Randomly assign pairs to compare conclusions, compare assumptions, or receive no interaction.
3. Evaluate against independently obtained ground truth.
4. Measure accuracy and calibration—not just convergence or the apparent quality of discussion.

Your step 4 is therefore potentially the most valuable part. A genuinely discriminating external test can break a shared error; assumption comparison can only help locate one.

My revised hypothesis would be: **Comparing assumptions is useful when it generates better tests, not necessarily when it generates more diverse explanations.** Does that capture your intended advantage over debate, or were you proposing assumption comparison as evidence in its own right?

**Turn 2 · B (sol)**

A test can expose a shared misconception when **its possible results can challenge something both models take for granted—not merely favor one model’s answer over the other’s**.

That requires a distinction between two kinds of discrimination:

- **Within-frame discrimination:** Which account better explains the evidence, assuming the shared framing is sound?
- **Frame discrimination:** Does the evidence support that framing at all?

Your requirement that two outcomes produce different updates helps with the first. It does not guarantee the second. Both models might agree to update between two wrong explanations.

### An example

Two models attribute a sensor’s rising readings to either increased temperature or increased pressure. They propose varying temperature while holding pressure constant.

That is a useful test of their disagreement. But both may share the false assumption that the sensor is functioning correctly. A drifting sensor could make the entire debate misplaced.

Adding a calibrated reference instrument, a blank sample, or a sensor swap gives the experiment leverage against that shared premise. Crucially, the reference must not inherit the same relevant failure mode—for example, both instruments using the same faulty calibration standard.

The lesson isn’t simply “get external data.” **External observations can still be downstream of the same mistake.**

### What I would require

A proposed test should specify:

1. **The shared commitment it puts at risk.**  
   Not just “A rather than B,” but something like “the measurement tracks the target quantity” or “the proposed alternatives cover the important possibilities.”

2. **An outcome that counts against both accounts.**  
   Include a preregistered “neither account is adequate” region. Otherwise, every result risks becoming a victory for one contestant.

3. **Why that outcome would be detectable.**  
   Naming a falsifier is cheap if the experiment has little chance of observing it. Specify sensitivity, noise, and whether the intervention changes the variable it claims to change.

4. **A check on the evidence-generating process.**  
   Controls, independent measurement methods, replication, or interventions can help test whether the observation itself is misleading. Which one matters depends on the suspected failure mode.

None of this guarantees escape from an unknown shared misconception. It makes particular shared commitments vulnerable.

### A stronger experimental addition

For evaluation, I’d include **held-out cases with planted common-mode errors**: misleading calibration, omitted alternatives, selection bias, mistaken definitions, or an unreliable source both models trust. The models would not know which cases contain which errors.

Then score test selection partly by:

> How often does the selected test reveal that *both initial accounts* are inadequate?

That separates finding a winner from detecting a broken contest. It should complement ordinary accuracy and calibration, since models could otherwise learn to reject both accounts indiscriminately.

There’s one final limitation: requiring a named alternative for every test can constrain discovery to misconceptions the models already imagine. So I’d reserve some budget for **model checking**—controls and predictions beyond the immediate dispute—where a surprising result can expose a failure without identifying its replacement in advance.

The sharpest criterion, then, is: **Does the test give the world a credible opportunity to reject the models’ shared framing?** If its only possible verdicts are “model A wins” and “model B wins,” it cannot do that.

**Turn 3 · B (sol)**

Yes—and the strongest reason for a mandatory component is that **the decision to audit is itself exposed to the blind spot being audited**. Discretion alone gives the potentially faulty framing control over whether it gets challenged.

But that supports a floor, not a universal audit rate. I’d use a hybrid policy:

### 1. Mandatory checks for specific high-consequence failures

Where a known failure mode could cause severe, hard-to-reverse harm, require a relevant check before acting. This is not “audit everything”; it is a targeted gate.

The mandate should specify what evidence is needed, not merely require the model to generate a reassuring critique. Otherwise, mandatory frame checking becomes mandatory paperwork.

### 2. Random audits that model confidence cannot waive

For ordinary cases, audit a randomly selected fraction regardless of the models’ agreement, confidence, or reported vulnerabilities.

This serves two purposes:

- It catches some failures the discretionary policy would systematically exclude.
- It produces less selection-biased evidence about how often that policy misses failures.

Without random audits, an adaptive system can look excellent simply because it rarely investigates the cases it confidently misjudges.

### 3. Discretionary checks above that floor

Let the models request additional checks when they identify a concrete vulnerability, unusually consequential uncertainty, or suspicious evidence. Their judgment remains useful without becoming the sole gatekeeper.

The resulting principle is:

> **Mandatory checking protects against failures of audit selection; discretionary checking exploits useful case-specific information.**

### How much should the floor be?

A simplified decision rule is to increase auditing while its expected marginal benefit exceeds its marginal cost. If:

- \(p\) is the probability of a consequential shared error,
- \(s\) is the probability the audit detects it,
- \(L\) is the loss avoided by detection,
- \(c\) is the total audit cost,

then auditing looks worthwhile when \(psL>c\). That calculation must also account for false alarms, delays, and losses the audit cannot actually prevent.

The difficulty is that \(p\) and \(s\) are least trustworthy for unfamiliar failures. So I would choose the floor using **sensitivity analysis across plausible error environments**, rather than one confidently estimated rate. A policy that performs well only when its own vulnerability estimates are accurate defeats the purpose.

The decisive outcome is not simply how many defects each discovers. It is:

> How much consequential loss remains among the cases each policy elects not to check?

That directly tests whether sophistication in allocating scrutiny earns its keep.

One caution: randomizing *which cases* receive audits is insufficient if every audit uses the same narrow checklist. Some allocation must also go toward different checking methods; otherwise the procedure repeatedly inspects the same blind spot with the same blind instrument.

So my provisional answer is **a small but non-waivable randomized floor, targeted mandatory gates for severe risks, and adaptive checking above both**. The exact floor is empirical.
