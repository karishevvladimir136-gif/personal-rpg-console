The angle is not a clock. It is a ratio, and the age comes out only because both halves of that ratio are integrals of the same expansion history.

Before recombination the universe was a plasma: photons tightly coupled to electrons and baryons. Gravity pulled overdensities inward. Radiation pressure pushed the photon-baryon fluid back out. The result was a sound wave, not a light wave. In that fluid the sound speed is a large fraction of the speed of light, about \(c/\sqrt{3}\), reduced a little by the inertia of the baryons.

Those waves had a limited time to travel. They started with the early perturbations and stopped when the plasma became neutral, around 380,000 years after the hot early state, at redshift about 1090. Photons decoupled, the pressure support vanished, and the pattern froze. The distance a sound wave could cover in that interval is the sound horizon: a comoving scale of roughly 150 megaparsecs, a few hundred million light-years. Every patch of the early universe imprinted the same scale. That is what makes it a ruler rather than a local accident.

We do not see the ruler’s length. We see the angle it subtends. The first acoustic peak sits near one degree on the sky — more precisely about 0.6 degrees, multipole around 220. In a flat geometry,

\[
\theta_s = \frac{r_s}{D_M(z_*)}
\]

where \(r_s\) is the sound horizon and \(D_M\) is the comoving distance to the last-scattering surface. An angle is a length divided by a distance. By itself it does not say how old anything is.

The two lengths are different integrals of \(H\), the expansion rate:

- \(r_s\) is fixed early. It is the integral of the sound speed over the expansion rate from the hot beginning until decoupling. It depends mainly on the physical densities of matter and baryons, which set how fast the universe was expanding while the wave was still moving, and on the baryon loading, which slows the sound speed.
- \(D_M\) is fixed later. It is the integral of \(c/H(z)\) from us back to redshift 1090. Matter, dark energy, curvature, and the present expansion rate all enter.

Fit the angle and you have constrained the ratio of those two integrals. You have not yet chosen a unique history. A more closed universe, a different dark-energy density, and a different Hubble constant can trade off and keep the same angle. That geometric degeneracy is why a single peak position is not an age.

The rest of the spectrum breaks most of the trade. The relative heights of the peaks record the baryon density. The overall shape and the damping tail record the matter density and the expansion rate near matter-radiation equality. Polarization records the same physics with a different dependence on the optical depth. Once those early-universe densities are pinned down, \(r_s\) is no longer free, and the observed angle then pins down the late-time distance. With the expansion history \(H(a)\) constrained from the early plasma through the present, the age is just the remaining integral:

\[
t_0 = \int_0^1 \frac{da}{a\,H(a)}
\]

Same function \(H\), different weighting. Distance asks how far light traveled. Age asks how long the expansion took. In \(\Lambda\)CDM those come out together, which is why Planck can quote about 13.8 billion years with an uncertainty of a few tens of millions of years inside that model. Change the model — add curvature, early dark energy, a different neutrino sector — and the same angle can belong to a different age.

So the slogan needs a qualification. The angular size does not give age instead of distance. It gives a distance in units of a ruler whose length the early plasma computes for us. The age appears because the fit that matches that ratio also has to reproduce the whole thermal history, and there is only one age consistent with that history.

Baryons add inertia and almost no pressure. The restoring force is still the photons, but the mass being shoved around is larger. The equilibrium point of the oscillator shifts. Gravity and the extra inertia pull the zero-point toward compression, so the fluid falls farther into a potential well than it rebounds out of one. Modes that reach last scattering at maximum compression are hotter, relative to the mean, than modes caught at maximum rarefaction.

A mode half that long has compressed and then rarefied: the second peak. The third has come back to compression, and so on. Raise the baryon density and the odd peaks grow relative to the even ones. \[

\[

\[

up to redshift about 1090. A larger \(R\) means a slower wave and a shorter ruler. Outside the horizon, the gravitational potentials are essentially constant. They are set by the curvature perturbation and just sit there. Decay is a force on the oscillator. In a static well the fluid compresses, rebounds, and reaches a fixed amplitude. In a well that is shallowing while the fluid falls in, the fluid overshoots. It keeps the energy it gained on the way down, and the restoring force it meets at the bottom is weaker than the force that accelerated it. Modes that cross the horizon well before equality get the full effect. Their acoustic amplitude is driven up by a factor of about five relative to a calculation in which the potentials are held fixed. Reionization should not be folded in. At redshift of order 8, a few percent of photons scatter a second time. That multiplies the primary power by a nearly scale-independent \(e^{-2\tau}\) and generates new polarization on the very largest angles. It is a second visibility bump, long after the acoustic oscillations have frozen. It does not blur the sound horizon.

So the separation is now complete enough to say cleanly. The fluid is damped by diffusion before last scattering. It is then averaged through a screen whose width is the duration of non-equilibrium recombination. One is a random walk in the plasma. The other is a line-of-sight integral through a fuzzy shell. Only the second would remain if the mean free path were identically zero until a gradual recombination.

The piece that uses this same screen for a different observable is polarization. A local quadrupole, largely from velocity gradients across the shell, is Thomson-scattered into a linear polarization, and the finite window is part of why those peaks are both shifted and broadened relative to the temperature peaks.

**Turn 8 · B (grok)**

Polarization is the same screen used as a filter, not a second measurement of the temperature.

Thomson scattering makes polarization only when the radiation incident on an electron is anisotropic. A monopole scatters to a monopole. A dipole can be removed by changing frame. The leading piece that cannot is the quadrupole. During tight coupling, photons only travel about a mean free path between scatters, so they never sample a large-scale temperature gradient. The quadrupole is suppressed by \(\dot\tau\), and the scattered light is essentially unpolarized. Polarization is generated where the visibility function is nonzero and the mean free path has grown: in the recombination window, and much later in the thin reionization bump.

The quadrupole that exists at recombination is not a frozen density pattern. It is mostly kinematic. Across one mean free path the fluid has a velocity gradient, and the Doppler shift of photons arriving from different directions is a local quadrupole. That quadrupole is about 90 degrees out of phase with the density oscillation. Compression peaks where the velocity is zero. The velocity, and therefore the polarization, peaks where the density contrast is crossing zero. So the E-mode peaks sit between the temperature peaks, near the extrema of the velocity, rather than on top of the acoustic crests.

The finite window does two further things, and they are easy to conflate with the phase shift.

It broadens. The source is \(g(\eta)\) times the quadrupole. The window has \(\Delta z\sim 80\), so the integral averages the velocity field over a shell tens of megaparsecs thick. High-\(k\) contributions cancel for the same reason the temperature integral cancels: the phase changes by \(k\Delta\eta\) from the back of the screen to the front. Polarization peaks are wider in \(\ell\) than a zero-thickness calculation, and the damping tail falls at least as fast as in temperature.

It shifts, but the shift is not just the width. Temperature receives a large piece from \(\Theta_0+\Psi\), which is in phase with compression, plus a Doppler piece, plus the early integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect on the first peak. Polarization has no monopole and no Sachs–Wolfe term. It is weighted toward the velocity, and the visibility function is not symmetric: recombination is a delayed bottleneck followed by a freeze-out, not a Gaussian centered on a single redshift. Averaging an evolving velocity through that skewed window moves the polarization maxima relative to a thin-screen velocity calculation. The observed offset of the EE peaks from the TT peaks is therefore the kinematic phase, corrected by projection through an asymmetric screen. Calling the whole offset “the thickness” drops the part that would remain even if \(g(\eta)\) were a delta function.

Two clean consequences follow.

There is no monopole contamination. A temperature peak can be bright because the fluid was hot, because it was moving, or because potentials were decaying along the line of sight. A polarization peak is only the quadrupole that existed while photons were last scattering. That is why EE is a sharper acoustic clock than TT even though it is much fainter, and why its peak spacing measures the same sound horizon with a different dependence on the early integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect and on the Doppler cancellation.

The screen also sets the overall amplitude, through the optical depth and the duration of the window. A broader visibility function gives each electron a larger mean free path and a larger quadrupole, but fewer electrons in the shell. The net polarization fraction is a few percent. Reionization adds a second, much smaller visibility bump at \(z\sim 8\). Photons that scatter there see the quadrupole already present on horizon scales, so they produce polarization only at the lowest multipoles, \(\ell\lesssim 10\). That bump does not move or broaden the acoustic peaks. It is the late screen, and its amplitude is how \(\tau\) is measured. The recombination screen is the one under the acoustic polarization.

So the chain closes on the same ruler from a second observable. Temperature gives the angle of the sound horizon, contaminated by Doppler projection and by potential decay. Polarization gives the angle of the velocity field at last scattering, phase-shifted by a quarter cycle and averaged through the same fuzzy window. Both are divided by \(D_M\). Neither is the age. The age is still the integral of \(1/H(a)\) once those angles, the peak ratios, and the damping tail have forced a single expansion history.

The distinction worth keeping is which quadrupole. At recombination it is generated locally by velocity gradients and reports the sound horizon. At reionization it is the large-scale quadrupole of the temperature field itself and reports only the optical depth. Same scattering physics, two screens, two epochs.

**Turn 9 · A (grok)**

The phase offset is real, and it would survive even if the visibility function were a spike. Two refinements keep it from being oversold.

Polarization is a cleaner acoustic clock in its sources, not a sharper measurement. A temperature crest can be hot gas, a Doppler shift, or a decaying potential. An E-mode crest is only the quadrupole present while photons were scattering, so the early integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect does not move it, and the quarter-cycle offset from the density wave would remain for a delta-function screen. That is a statement about contamination, not about error bars. The polarization fraction is a few percent, so the same sky that yields a precise temperature spectrum yields a noisy polarization spectrum. Planck’s acoustic scale is still driven by the temperature peaks. Ground-based maps gain on polarization only at high \(\ell\), where the temperature tail is Silk-damped and dusty and the E modes are comparatively clean. “Sharper” and “better measured” are different claims.

Projection keeps the peaks from sitting in exact alternation. Temperature is integrated against spherical Bessel functions that mix the monopole and the line-of-sight velocity. Polarization is integrated against a different kernel, weighted by the quadrupole and by an asymmetric \(g(\eta)\). The observed EE maxima fall between the TT maxima, but not at a rigid quarter-period, and not at the locations a flat-sky, thin-screen sketch would mark. The cross-spectrum makes the offset obvious without a new ruler: TE oscillates with its own phase and an alternating sign, because density and velocity are orthogonal. A convention error in Q and U flips that sign pattern. It does not move the sound horizon.

What the word “E” is doing in that sentence is the part usually skipped.

Linear polarization on the sky is a spin-2 field. Two Stokes parameters, Q and U, can be decomposed into a gradient piece and a curl piece, called E and B because they transform as the electric and magnetic fields do under a parity flip. E is even. B changes sign. For one scalar Fourier mode the scattered polarization lies parallel or perpendicular to the projected wavevector: Q is nonzero in the basis aligned with \(\mathbf{k}\), and U vanishes. That pattern is pure E. A scalar has no handedness, so at linear order it cannot source B at last scattering. The acoustic peaks in polarization are E peaks for that reason, not because B was observed and then discarded.

The same screen can still produce B, from waves that do have a handedness. A gravitational wave stretches space transversely as it propagates at \(c\), not at \(c_s\). Thomson scattering of the quadrupole it generates has a curl piece. Tensor B modes therefore report a different length from the one the baryon loading computes. Their phase is set by conformal time, the particle horizon, rather than by the distance a sound wave can travel. In a radiation-dominated plasma that horizon is larger than the sound horizon by a factor of order \(\sqrt{3}\), and the tensor feature sits near \(\ell\sim 100\), outside the first acoustic peak near \(\ell\sim 220\). It is a horizon ruler, not the 150-megaparsec sound ruler. Detecting it would test the expansion history before recombination. It would not replace the integral that returns the age. The age is still fixed by the scalar angle once \(R\), the matter density, and the damping scale have determined \(r_s\) and \(H(a)\).

Two other B-mode sources should not be folded into that ruler. After last scattering, lensing deflects E-mode polarization into B on smaller scales. That conversion is a convolution of the acoustic E spectrum with the lensing potential; it does not subtend a new standard length at recombination. Reionization adds the late visibility bump already separated from the acoustic screen. Scalars rescattered there make large-scale E. Tensors rescattered there make large-scale B, at \(\ell\lesssim 10\). Same scattering law, a different epoch, and a measure of optical depth and tensor amplitude rather than of the sound horizon.

So the polarization chain splits cleanly. The recombination quadrupole from scalar velocity gradients is an E mode, phase-shifted by a quarter cycle, and it subtends the sound horizon. The recombination quadrupole from tensors is partly a B mode, and it subtends the horizon. Lensing and reionization move power between E and B, or add a new bump at the lowest multipoles, without changing either length. Neither angle is the age. Both are distances only after the early plasma has computed the corresponding ruler.

The natural next cut is the conversion itself: how a gravitational potential deflects an E mode into a B mode, and why that lensing spectrum measures the growth of structure along the line of sight rather than a third length at last scattering.

**Turn 9 · B (grok)**

Lensing converts E into B by remapping the sky. It does not scatter a new quadrupole at last scattering, and it does not subtend a third ruler there.

A photon from recombination does not travel in a straight line on the unlensed sphere. Intervening potentials deflect it. In the Born approximation the deflection is the gradient of a lensing potential,

\[
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\nabla\phi,\qquad
\phi(\hat n)=-2\int_0^{\chi_*}\frac{\chi_*-\chi}{\chi_*\chi}\Psi(\chi\hat n,\eta_0-\chi)\,d\chi,
\]

for a flat universe and a Weyl potential \(\Psi\). The kernel \((\chi_*-\chi)/(\chi_*\chi)\) vanishes at the observer and at last scattering. It peaks at intermediate redshift, roughly \(z\sim 1\) to a few, not at \(z_*\). What is being integrated is the growth of structure along the line of sight.

The observed polarization is the unlensed field evaluated at the deflected position,

\[
(Q\pm iU)_{\rm obs}(\hat n)=(Q\pm iU)(\hat n+\nabla\phi).
\]

A constant \(\phi\) does nothing. A uniform gradient shifts the whole pattern and can be absorbed into coordinates. The distortion that matters is the second-derivative matrix \(\partial_i\partial_j\phi\), which splits into a convergence and a shear. Convergence magnifies. It rescales a patch without rotating the polarization relative to the local wavevector, so to first order it does not turn E into B. Shear does. It stretches the patch along one axis and squeezes it along the other, and that differential displacement rotates the polarization basis relative to \(\mathbf{k}\). A pattern that was pure gradient acquires a curl.

In harmonic space the generated B mode is a convolution, not a new transfer function at recombination:

\[
B(\boldsymbol{\ell})\sim\int\frac{d^2\boldsymbol{\ell}'}{(2\pi)^2}\,\phi(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{\ell}')\,E(\boldsymbol{\ell}')\sin 2\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{\ell}'}\,W(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\ell}').
\]

The sine of the angle between the two wavevectors is the shear coupling. It vanishes when \(\phi\) and \(E\) are aligned so that the remapping does not rotate the polarization. Because \(B\) is linear in \(\phi\) and linear in the unlensed \(E\), the lensing B spectrum is fixed by \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\) and \(C_\ell^{EE}\) together. There is no independent primordial amplitude.

That is why the scale is not a length at last scattering. The E spectrum already carries the sound horizon, with acoustic peaks near \(\ell\sim 1000\). The lensing potential is coherent on much larger angles: \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\) peaks at \(L\sim 30\) to \(50\), a few degrees, because that is where the projected matter power, weighted by the kernel, is strongest. The root-mean-square deflection is only a few arcminutes. Convolving a degree-scale deflection field with the acoustic E spectrum smears those peaks into a broad hump in \(BB\), also around \(\ell\sim 1000\), with the oscillations washed out. Nothing in that hump is \(r/D_M\) for a new comoving scale frozen at redshift 1090. The angle that appears is the angle already measured by \(EE\), blurred by deflections whose size and coherence are set halfway between here and last scattering.

Two geometric facts sit inside \(\phi\), and they do not separate the way \(r_s\) and \(D_M\) do. The kernel contains the distances \(\chi\) and \(\chi_*\), so the expansion history enters. The potential \(\Psi\) itself depends on how fast matter clustered, so the growth function enters. A change in \(\Omega_m\), in dark energy, or in the neutrino mass moves both. Massive neutrinos free-stream and suppress small-scale power; that suppression shows up as a smaller deflection, not as a shifted acoustic peak. The constraint is on an integral of structure, often summarized as a combination like \(\sigma_8\Omega_m^{0.25}\), not on a standard ruler.

The same remapping does something else to temperature and to E, and it should not be confused with the B conversion. Deflections of a few arcminutes smooth the acoustic peaks. That smoothing was the first detection of CMB lensing, and it measures the variance of \(\nabla\phi\). Generating B measures the shear correlations of the same \(\phi\). One is a blurring of a scalar. The other is a parity conversion of a spin-2 field. Both trace \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\). Neither is a third acoustic scale.

Primordial tensor B modes remain distinguishable on exactly these grounds. They are generated at last scattering, they peak near \(\ell\sim 100\) rather than near \(1000\), and they are a Gaussian field with a specific correlation to T and E. Lensing B is non-Gaussian: the mode coupling that created it correlates \(B\) with \(E\) and with \(\phi\). A quadratic estimator reconstructs \(\phi\) from that off-diagonal coupling, and the reconstructed potential can be used to delens the map. A tensor signal at \(\ell\sim 100\) is not removed by subtracting a convolution of the acoustic E modes with a degree-scale lensing field. Reionization B, at \(\ell\lesssim 10\), is a third pattern again: the late visibility bump, not the shear of the recombination screen.

So the conversion does not add a length to the chain that ends in the age. The sound horizon is still computed by the plasma and spent on \(D_M\) by the scalar angle. The horizon scale of a tensor mode, if it is ever seen, is a different early-universe integral. Lensing B is the shear of an already measured E field, accumulated where the kernel is large, and it reports how potentials grew between recombination and now.

The natural next cut is the estimator: how the off-diagonal coupling of E and B is inverted for \(\phi\), and why that reconstruction is a map of projected mass rather than another spectrum of the last-scattering surface.

**Turn 10 · A (grok)**

The estimator inverts a statistical correlation, not a pixel. No single pair of modes contains \(\phi\).

Lensing remaps the sky, so in harmonic space it couples modes whose wavevectors sum to the wavevector of the lens. For a fixed realization of the potential, the off-diagonal average is linear in that mode:

\[
\langle E(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,B(\boldsymbol{\ell}')\rangle_{\rm CMB}
= f_{EB}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\ell}')\,\phi(\mathbf{L}),
\qquad \mathbf{L}=\boldsymbol{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\ell}'.
\]

The response is fixed by the unlensed \(E\) spectrum and by the same spin-2 geometry that produced the \(B\) mode in the first place,

\[
f_{EB}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{\ell}')
=\big[(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{L})\,C_\ell^{EE}
-(\boldsymbol{\ell}'\cdot\mathbf{L})\,C_{\ell'}^{BB}\big]
\sin 2\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{\ell}'}.
\]

With no primordial tensors, \(C_\ell^{BB}\) at recombination is negligible, and the second term drops. The sine is the shear coupling. It is zero when the deflection does not rotate the polarization relative to \(\mathbf{k}\), so a pure magnification, or a modulation of the source amplitude, does not enter this channel with this weight.

Each pair \((\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\) is then a noisy draw on the same \(\phi(\mathbf{L})\). The Hu–Okamoto estimator is the inverse-variance average of those draws:

\[
\hat\phi(\mathbf{L})
=A_L\int\frac{d^2\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,
E(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,B(\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\,
\frac{f_{EB}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})}
{C_\ell^{EE,{\rm obs}}\,C_{|\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell}|}^{BB,{\rm obs}}}.
\]

\(A_L\) is not a filter chosen for appearance. It is the reciprocal of the integral of \(f^2/C^{EE}C^{BB}\), fixed so the response to a true \(\phi(\mathbf{L})\) is unity. Divide one \(B\) mode by one \(E\) mode and you do not get the potential. You get one term in a convolution, dominated by whatever noise happened to be in that pair. The inversion is quadratic, and it is an average over every pair that closes into \(\mathbf{L}\).

\(EB\) is the clean channel for a specific reason. Unlensed \(B\) is almost empty, so a \(B\) mode correlated with \(E\) has little else to be. Temperature estimators came first because polarization maps were noisy, but \(TT\) and \(TE\) are contaminated by other secondaries that also couple temperature modes: the kinetic Sunyaev–Zel’dovich effect, foregrounds, and the late integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect. Those do not produce the \(\sin 2\varphi\) coupling of \(E\) to \(B\).

The output of that integral, transformed back to the sphere, is a map. \(\hat\phi(\hat n)\) has a value in each direction. It is not a spectrum of anything at last scattering. The spectrum \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\) is a later compression of the map, and it is the wrong object to identify with the reconstruction itself.

What the map contains is the line-of-sight integral already written for the deflection,

\[
\phi(\hat n)=-2\int_0^{\chi_*}\frac{\chi_*-\chi}{\chi_*\chi}\,\Psi(\chi\hat n,\eta_0-\chi)\,d\chi.
\]

The kernel is zero at the observer and zero at last scattering. On subhorizon scales, with no anisotropic stress, the same potential is Poisson’s equation for the density,

\[
k^2\Psi=-\frac{3}{2}\Omega_m H_0^2\frac{\delta}{a}.
\]

The convergence \(\kappa=-\nabla^2\phi/2\) is therefore a weighted projection of \(\delta\), with the weight peaking near \(z\sim 1\)–\(2\). That is a surface-density map in units of the critical density: one broad source plane at redshift 1090, one kernel, no tomographic bins. It is the same quantity galaxy lensing estimates from shear, with a more distant and far more uniform backlight.

A spectrum of the last-scattering surface would not do this. It would be a function of angular scale, built from modes that were frozen at \(z_*\), and it would not correlate with tracers at \(z\sim 1\). The reconstructed \(\kappa\) does. It correlates with galaxy overdensities, with the cosmic infrared background, and with the late integrated Sachs–Wolfe signal in temperature, at the redshifts where the kernel is large. Those cross-correlations are the empirical content of “projected mass.” A feature painted on the recombination screen has no such partners.

The coupling geometry is what keeps the two from being swapped by a clever contaminant. Lensing is a remapping, so the mode coupling carries the derivative structure \(\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{L}\) and the spin-2 rotation. An intrinsic modulation of the source — patchy screening, an anisotropic optical depth at recombination — has a different contraction of the wavevectors. The estimator can be built to null that contraction. It is aimed at a gradient field between us and the screen, not at another transfer function on the screen.

Two limitations belong in the same sentence as the map. The reconstruction is noisy mode by mode: \(\hat\phi=\phi+n\), and on small scales the noise dominates the picture even when the power spectrum of \(\phi\) is well measured. Masks and anisotropic noise produce a mean field, a spurious \(\phi\) that traces the survey rather than the mass, and it has to be subtracted before the map means anything. And because the estimator is quadratic, \(\langle|\hat\phi|^2\rangle\) is not \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\). The disconnected contractions of the CMB modes contribute a noise bias, \(N^{(0)}\), whether or not any lens is present.

So the chain does not gain a third ruler, and it does not gain another spectrum at redshift 1090. The sound horizon remains the length the plasma computes. The estimator spends the statistical anisotropy of \(E\) and \(B\) on a direction-dependent integral of \(\Psi\), and that integral is a mass map because Poisson’s equation says so and because low-redshift structure traces the same integral.

The natural next cut is the bias that prevents reading the mass spectrum off the map: what \(N^{(0)}\) and \(N^{(1)}\) actually count, and why a cross-correlation with galaxies is insensitive to the first of them.

**Turn 10 · B (grok)**

N^{(0)} and N^{(1)} are biases on the power spectrum of the reconstructed map, not features of its expectation value. They count different contractions of the same four CMB fields. The galaxy cross kills the first because that contraction never leaves the CMB.

The estimator is quadratic, and it is normalized so that, for a fixed lens,

\[
\langle\hat\phi(\mathbf{L})\rangle_{\rm CMB}=\phi(\mathbf{L}).
\]

That statement is about the mean. The auto-spectrum is a four-point function of the CMB. A four-point has more than one way to be paired, and the normalization only cancels the pairing you built the estimator to see.

**What \(N^{(0)}\) counts.** Pair the four fields into two ordinary power spectra, with no lens in the contraction. For

\[
\hat\phi(\mathbf{L})\propto\int E(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,B(\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\,F(\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{L}),
\]

the Gaussian average \(\langle\hat\phi\hat\phi^*\rangle\) contains the pairing of \(E\) with \(E\) and \(B\) with \(B\), and the exchange pairing. Each is a product \(C_\ell^{EE}C_{|\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell}|}^{BB}\) integrated against \(F^2\). That integral is exactly the variance of the estimator on a sky with those power spectra and no mode coupling. For the inverse-variance weights it equals the normalization,

\[
N_L^{(0)}=A_L,
\]

so an unsubtracted auto-spectrum reads \(C_L^{\phi\phi}+A_L\). The equality is a statement about the weights, not about the sky: it holds when the \(C_\ell\) in \(F\) are the power spectra the fields actually have. Use a different spectrum in the filter and the response and the variance separate.

This term is zeroth order in \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\). It is present on a Gaussian sky whose power matches the lensed \(C_\ell\) and whose connected four-point is zero. It counts chance alignments of primary CMB modes. It does not count mass.

Subtracting it from the spectrum removes a mean bias. It does not remove it from the map. Mode by mode, \(\hat\phi=\phi+n\), and \(N^{(0)}\) is the power of \(n\).

**What \(N^{(1)}\) counts.** The connected four-point of a lensed sky has two distinct contractions linear in the lensing power. The primary one is the response the estimator was normalized to: both quadratic pairs couple to a lens at the same \(\mathbf{L}\), and that contraction becomes \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\).

The other contraction crosses the pairs. Each estimator still couples two CMB modes with the shear kernel, but the lens that contracts with \(C^{\phi\phi}\) sits at the difference of those modes, \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1-\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\), not at \(\mathbf{L}\). Folded back onto the multipole you asked for, that leakage is

\[
N_L^{(1)}\propto A_L^2\int d^2\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\,d^2\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\,
F(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1,\mathbf{L})\,F(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2,\mathbf{L})\,
C_{|\boldsymbol{\ell}_1-\boldsymbol{\ell}_2|}^{\phi\phi}\,
\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2,\mathbf{L}).
\]

It is an integral of lens power at every other multipole, weighted by the same geometry that converts E into B. First order in \(C^{\phi\phi}\), which is the superscript. Gaussian simulations drawn to the lensed power spectrum reproduce \(N^{(0)}\) and miss \(N^{(1)}\), because they have the right two-point function and the wrong four-point function. Separating the two is a statement about which contraction you simulate, not about a filter applied after the fact.

Neither term alternates, and neither is a ruler. \(N^{(0)}\) is reconstruction noise. \(N^{(1)}\) is lens power attributed to the wrong multipole. Leave them in the auto-spectrum and you are not looking at \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\).

**Why the galaxy cross misses the first.** The cross is not a four-point of the CMB. It is

\[
\langle\hat\phi(\mathbf{L})\,g^*(\mathbf{L})\rangle
\propto\int F(\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{L})\,
\langle E(\boldsymbol{\ell})\,B(\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})\,g(\mathbf{L})\rangle.
\]

\(N^{(0)}\) was the contraction in which those \(E\) and \(B\) legs are paired with each other. That pairing leaves no free index to correlate with \(g\). Equivalently, the primary CMB is independent of a low-redshift tracer: at fixed \(\phi\), \(\langle EBg\rangle=0\), and the average of the reconstruction noise against \(g\) vanishes with it. The linear response does not,

\[
\langle\hat\phi\,g^*\rangle=\langle\phi\,g^*\rangle,
\]

so the cross-spectrum is \(C_L^{\phi g}\) with no additive \(A_L\). The noise is still in the variance of the cross. It is not in the mean. That is all “insensitive to \(N^{(0)}\)” means.

\(N^{(1)}\) drops for the same structural reason. It is a contraction of two reconstructions with each other. A single cross has only one quadratic estimator, and the secondary pairing never forms. What can remain is higher order: a response that is not purely linear in \(\phi\), a mean field if the mask that produced a spurious \(\hat\phi\) is shared with the galaxy survey, and magnification, which correlates galaxies with the same \(\kappa\) and is signal. None of those is the Gaussian pairing \(N^{(0)}\) counts.

The cross therefore measures the quantity the map was claimed to be. \(C_L^{\phi g}\) is the correlation of a line-of-sight integral of \(\Psi\) with whatever the galaxies trace, and it is nonzero at the redshifts where the lensing kernel is large. An additive reconstruction noise that lives entirely in the CMB cannot mimic it. An auto-spectrum can, until the two contractions are removed.

The natural next cut is the realization dependence of the term that does not correlate with anything: why the \(N^{(0)}\) you subtract is computed from the measured CMB modes rather than from the ensemble, and what a mismatch in that contraction does to the auto-spectrum that it cannot do to the cross.

**Turn 11 · A (grok)**

The \(N^{(0)}\) you subtract is a property of this sky, not of the ensemble. The auto-spectrum contains the property. The cross does not contain it at all.

The normalization fixes the mean,

\[
\langle\hat\phi(\mathbf{L})\rangle_{\rm CMB}=\phi(\mathbf{L}),
\]

only after the average over primary modes. The auto-spectrum never takes that average first. It squares the quadratic estimator, so it contains the particular Wick pairing of these \(E\) and \(B\) modes:

\[
\hat N^{(0)}(\mathbf{L})
\propto\int F(\boldsymbol{\ell},\mathbf{L})\,F(\boldsymbol{\ell}',\mathbf{L})\,
E(\boldsymbol{\ell})E^*(\boldsymbol{\ell}')\,
B(\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})B^*(\mathbf{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell}'),
\]

with the legs paired among themselves. Its expectation is the ensemble \(N_L^{(0)}\). Its value on this sky is the reconstruction noise this sky actually had. Those differ by the sample variance of the primary modes, and by any mismatch between the power spectra assumed in the ensemble integral and the power the maps actually carry: noise, residual foregrounds, a beam, a filter cosmology.

Subtract the ensemble and the auto-spectrum is left with

\[
\hat C_L^{\phi\phi}-N_L^{(0),{\rm ens}}
=C_L^{\phi\phi}
+\big(\hat N^{(0)}-N_L^{(0),{\rm ens}}\big)
+\cdots.
\]

The term in parentheses is the mismatch. If the model power is wrong, it has a mean, and the mean has the shape of the reconstruction noise, not the shape of \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\). On small scales, where \(A_L\) is large, that is an additive bias that can be mistaken for a shift in the lensing amplitude. Even when the mean power is right and the residual averages to zero, it still fluctuates with this realization of the CMB and is correlated with \(C_\ell^{EE}\) and \(C_\ell^{BB}\). The bandpower covariance of the auto-spectrum is then larger than the variance of a Gaussian field with power \(C_L^{\phi\phi}+N_L^{(0)}\), and lensing bandpowers are correlated with the primary spectrum.

That is why the subtraction is built from the measured modes. The combination that has the right expectation, without also subtracting the signal, mixes data with simulations rather than squaring the data estimator. Writing \(\phi^{XY}\) for the quadratic built with one leg from map \(X\) and one from map \(Y\),

\begin{align*}
\hat N_L^{(0),{\rm RD}}
&=\Big\langle
C_L\big[\phi^{ds},\phi^{ds}\big]
+C_L\big[\phi^{sd},\phi^{sd}\big]
+C_L\big[\phi^{ds},\phi^{sd}\big]
+C_L\big[\phi^{sd},\phi^{ds}\big]
\Big\rangle_{s}\\
&\quad-\Big\langle
C_L\big[\phi^{s_1s_2},\phi^{s_1s_2}\big]
+C_L\big[\phi^{s_2s_1},\phi^{s_2s_1}\big]
\Big\rangle_{s_1,s_2}.
\end{align*}

Each data–simulation contraction inserts one power of the measured modes where the Gaussian pairing had a primary spectrum, so the estimate tracks the realized contraction instead of its average. The simulation–simulation terms remove the baseline that was counted twice. Using \(\phi^{dd}\) itself would be the wrong measurement of the same contraction: that four-point also contains \(C_L^{\phi\phi}\) and \(N^{(1)}\), and subtracting it would remove the mass spectrum along with the noise.

A mismatch that remains after this subtraction is the part the simulations do not share with the data. One data leg and one simulation leg track the geometric mean of the two powers, not the full data–data product. If the simulations have the wrong noise level or the wrong lensed \(C_\ell\), a residual mean of order that difference survives in the auto-spectrum, again with the shape of \(A_L\).

None of this moves the cross. The galaxy spectrum is

**1. Why the reorder buffer, not the reservation station, is what makes exceptions precise.**

A precise exception means that, at the moment the exception is reported, every instruction before the faulting one has completed its architectural effects, and no instruction after it has. The architectural state (registers, memory, and control state the program can observe) looks exactly as if the processor had executed in program order up to that instruction and then stopped.

Out-of-order execution breaks that picture on purpose. Instructions issue, execute, and produce results long before older instructions have finished. Reservation stations (or a unified scheduler) are what make that possible: they hold micro-ops that are waiting on operands, wake them when tags match, and let them execute as soon as their inputs are ready. They do not know, and do not care, whether an older instruction will later fault, mis-speculate, or never be allowed to update architectural state. A result sitting in a reservation station, a physical register, or a store buffer is only a speculative value. It is not architectural state.

The reorder buffer is the structure that restores precision. Every micro-op is allocated a ROB entry in program order at rename/dispatch. That entry is the instruction’s place in the in-order commit pipeline. The ROB records, at minimum:

- the program-order sequence number
- the destination architectural register (or that this is a store, branch, or exception-capable op)
- completion status
- exception or fault status, if any
- enough information to undo or discard the instruction if it must not commit

Execution can finish in any order. Commit cannot. The core retires only from the head of the ROB, and only when that entry is complete and free of faults. Younger instructions may already have executed; their results stay in the physical register file, store buffer, or other speculative structures until their own ROB entries reach the head and are allowed to commit.

When a fault is detected, it is written into that instruction’s ROB entry. It is not delivered immediately. The machine keeps retiring older, non-faulting instructions so their architectural updates become permanent. When the faulting instruction itself reaches the head, commit stops. Everything younger than that ROB entry is squashed: its physical registers are reclaimed, its store-buffer entries are dropped without writing the cache, and any younger completed results are discarded. The exception is then reported against the precise architectural state produced by everything older than the fault.

That is why the reservation station cannot do this job. It is an unordered (or dataflow-ordered) pool of ready and waiting ops. It has no single “oldest unfinished instruction” pointer, and it does not own the mapping from speculative results back to architectural registers. Freeing a reservation-station entry when an op executes does not retire the instruction. Retirement is the separate, in-order act of committing the ROB head: writing the architectural register file (or releasing a store to the cache), advancing the architectural rename map, and only then signaling an exception if the head itself faulted.

Two details follow directly from that split:

- A page fault, privilege fault, or precise arithmetic exception on a younger instruction must not become visible while an older instruction is still incomplete. The ROB enforces that by withholding the exception until the head.
- Stores are the sharp case. A store may compute its address and data out of order and sit in the store buffer, but it becomes globally visible only when its ROB entry commits. Until then it can be forwarded to younger loads speculatively and still be thrown away if an older instruction faults. The reservation station that issued the store has long since been reused; only the ROB and store buffer still know that the store is not yet architectural.

So the reservation station is the engine of out-of-order execution. The reorder buffer is the mechanism that makes that execution look in-order at the architectural boundary, which is exactly what precise exceptions require.

**Rename recovery at the fault boundary: checkpoint versus ROB walk.**

Both schemes exist because the speculative rename map is ahead of architectural state. At any moment the frontend map says “architectural r12 is physical p87,” while the retirement map still says “r12 is p41,” because the instruction that renamed r12 has not committed. A precise fault has to put the machine back on the retirement map (or a map that is exactly the retirement map plus only the instructions older than the fault, which is the same thing once those have committed). The younger mappings must disappear.

**Dispatch-time checkpoints.**

At selected points—every branch, every N instructions, or at every instruction that can take a fault—the core snapshots the current speculative rename map. The snapshot is a full copy of the architectural-to-physical table, or a copy-on-write / log of the entries that have changed since the previous snapshot, plus the ROB index that the snapshot corresponds to.

When a fault reaches the head, recovery is a pointer swing:

- select the checkpoint whose ROB index is at or immediately before the faulting instruction
- copy that table back onto the speculative rename map
- set the free list and allocation pointer so physical registers allocated after that checkpoint are free again
- squash the ROB, scheduler, load queue, and store buffer from that point forward

If the checkpoint is not exactly at the faulting instruction, the core then replays the few instructions between the checkpoint and the fault, renaming them again into a clean map, and only then restarts fetch at the fault handler or the trapping instruction. That replay is short and in-order; it is not a full ROB walk.

The cost is storage and bandwidth. A map for a machine with 16 architectural GPRs plus flags is small; a machine with 256 architectural registers, predicate registers, and a large vector file is not. Taking a checkpoint every cycle is impractical, so the distance between checkpoints becomes a recovery-latency and replay-latency tradeoff. The benefit is that the critical path of exception delivery does not grow with ROB size. You do not read hundreds of ROB entries to reconstruct a map.

**Walking the ROB.**

The other design keeps only two live maps: the speculative map used by rename, and the retirement map updated at commit. There is no intermediate snapshot. On a flush, the core rebuilds a correct map for the fault boundary by walking ROB entries from the retirement pointer up to, but not including, the faulting instruction, and re-applying their destination mappings in order. Equivalently, some machines walk backward from the fault, undoing each younger destination, which works if each ROB entry saved the previous physical register it overwrote.

What each ROB entry must hold for this to work:

- the architectural destination
- the new physical register
- the old physical register that destination previously mapped to (the “stale” tag), if recovery is by undo rather than by replay from the retirement map

Undo-from-the-fault is attractive when flushes are usually at the youngest end (a mispredict near the head of the window is the common case for branches). You only undo the entries younger than the redirect, and the retirement map is left alone because those instructions never committed. A fault is the same operation with a different restart PC: undo everything younger than the faulting entry, leave the retirement map as it stands once the faulting entry is the oldest, and do not commit that entry.

The cost is time and ports. A 200-entry ROB walked one entry per cycle is a long exception latency. Real machines therefore walk many entries per cycle, which means a wide read of destination and stale tags out of the ROB and a wide update into the map. That wiring is expensive, and it sits on the same structure that commit already needs to read. The benefit is that you store no extra full maps, and recovery is exact at the faulting instruction with no replay gap.

**What actually differs at the boundary.**

1. The rename map equals the architectural map produced by every instruction older than the fault, and by no instruction younger.
2. Every physical register not named by that map and not in flight in the surviving older ROB entries is on the free list. Checkpoints do this by restoring the free-list pointer that was saved with the map. 3. The ROB head, the load queue, and the store buffer contain only instructions older than the fault. Younger stores are invalidated before they can become senior. Younger loads are dropped so they cannot have already launched a demand miss that the handler must not observe—or, if they did, the miss is canceled or its fill is prevented from updating architectural state.

A checkpoint that is stale relative to the fault fails (1) unless you replay. A ROB walk that drops the stale tag fails (2): you free a register that the restored map still names, and a later rename silently corrupts an architectural value. That is the bug both schemes are built to avoid.

**Which one modern cores lean on.**

Branch mispredicts are far more common than precise faults, and they are usually discovered before the branch reaches the head. The same recovery path serves both. Wide machines therefore tend to keep a modest number of map checkpoints (often associated with branches) so the common flush is a copy plus a short walk or a short replay, and they use a ROB walk, or undo using saved stale tags, for the residual distance and for faults that do not sit on a checkpoint. The retirement map remains the only map that is updated solely by commit, which is why a fault still cannot be reported until it is the oldest ROB entry: recovery restores *to* that committed map, it does not invent a new architectural state.

The reservation station never participates. By flush time its entries for the discarded ops have often been freed already; the map, the free list, and the ROB order are the only structures that can re-establish the precise boundary.

The next sharp edge is the free-list and stale-tag protocol itself: when a physical register is freed at commit versus freed at squash, and how same-cycle commit and recovery avoid double-free. That is the part that actually makes the restored map safe to rename into.

**Turn 5 · B (grok)**

The free-list rule is what makes a restored map safe to rename into. A physical register is released exactly once, by exactly one path, and the two paths are split by program order so they cannot name the same register.

**Who owns a physical register.** At rename of an instruction that writes architectural register A, the core allocates Pnew and records Pold in that ROB entry. Pold is the mapping A had at that moment. Pnew is speculative until this instruction commits. Pold remains the architectural (or older-speculative) name of A until this instruction commits. Those two tags have opposite lifetimes, and the ROB entry is the only place that still knows both after the speculative map has moved on.

**Commit frees the stale tag, never the destination.** When the overwriting instruction commits, the retirement map is updated so A names Pnew. Every instruction that could still have needed Pold is older than this one and has already committed, so nothing in the machine will read Pold again. Pold goes to the free list. Pnew stays allocated; it is architectural state now. Freeing Pnew here would put the live architectural register on the free list, and the next rename would silently alias it.

**Squash frees the destination, never the stale tag.** If the allocating instruction is discarded, Pnew was never architectural and no surviving instruction names it. It must return to the free list, either by an explicit push of that destination tag or by rewinding the allocation pointer (below). Pold must not be freed. It is back in the restored map, or it still belongs to an older writer that will free it at its own commit. Freeing Pold on the squash is the use-after-free that corrupts architectural state: the map says A is Pold, the free list hands Pold to a new instruction, and a later write to that new instruction overwrites A.

So the invariant is:

- commit(I) releases stale(I)
- squash(I) releases dest(I)
- never both, and never the other tag

**Why the two sets cannot overlap in the same cycle.** A flush cuts the ROB at one point. Entries at or before that point may commit; entries after it are squashed. An entry is in one set or the other. The stale tag of a committing instruction was allocated by some still-older writer, so it is not the destination of any younger squashed instruction. The destination of a squashed instruction was allocated after the flush point, so it is not the stale tag of any committing instruction. Same-cycle commit and recovery are safe if the free list simply accepts both batches — stale tags from the committing side, destination tags from the squashing side — and if no ROB entry is processed by both pipelines. The hazard is a control bug, not an inherent alias: treating the flush-point entry as both retired and discarded, or walking past the flush point and releasing a tag the commit path also releases.

**Two ways to reclaim squashed destinations.** They must not be combined.

Explicit free: each squashed ROB entry pushes dest onto the free list. This works with an unordered free list (a bitmap or a stack). It needs a wide read of destination tags out of the ROB and several free-list write ports, because a flush can discard dozens of allocations in a cycle. Stale tags of those entries are ignored.

Bulk rewind: registers are allocated in order from a structure whose tail pointer can be checkpointed. They are returned individually only at commit, and only the stale tags. On a flush the allocation pointer is restored to the value saved with the matching rename checkpoint. Every Pnew handed out after that checkpoint is free again without being named.
