````text
It comes to him all at once, the way old things sometimes do.

He has held a coin like this before, twelve years ago in the Tethyrian hills during the war. His scout troop sheltered from the rain in a barrow, and the dead inside had these on their eyes: the same mould seam, the same heavy cast gold, the same crowned and bearded face. The old sergeant who pocketed them wouldn't spend them. He called them *under-hill gold*. He said they were older than any crown in Tethyr or Amn, left by men who learned their letters from the dwarves and later followed the dwarves into the ground. Nobody in that troop knew what became of those people. The sergeant didn't either.

Now that he's looking for it, James can see why the letters aren't dwarvish. The strokes are Dethek, cut the way a dwarf cuts them, but they don't make dwarvish words. Read as Dwarvish they're nonsense. Somebody used dwarven letters to write a language of their own.

He sees three more things the smith and the moneychanger missed.

First, the earth packed into the letters isn't forest loam or field dirt. It's grey and gritty and fine as flour, with flecks that catch the light: the rock dust that cakes a miner's boots a long way down. It's laced with rust-red that comes off on his thumbnail. This coin lay against iron for a long time, and the iron was old enough to rot.

Second, there's a mark on the reverse, inside the ring of letters, small enough to hide under a fingertip. It was punched into the metal after casting: a circle with a single straight bar through it. Whatever it is, it isn't part of the coin's design. Someone added it later, the way an assayer marks good metal or a temple marks what belongs to it. He doesn't know the sign.

Third, the coin is cold. That shouldn't matter, since it's morning and metal holds the night's chill. But he has been holding it for some time now, it hasn't warmed in his palm at all, and his hand is starting to ache faintly at the bones.

Across the street, Penny has stopped washing. She sits up on the inn step, ears forward, looking at his hand.

Fuiruim has been watching James's face, not the coin. He's seen it change.

"You know it," he says. It's not a question. "Well? And if we deal on the other three, we weigh them at Hendak's scale in the market, in front of people. I'll not have it said Fuiruim passed bad gold to a stranger at his own gate."
````
