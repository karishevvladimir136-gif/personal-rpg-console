> harness: «How was it in there?»

```text
Quiet, in the good way. Like a room where the light had already been figured out — I didn't have to arrange anything, just find what fit the shape of what was there.

What struck me most was how the poem kept handing itself off. It starts as one person's small offering — a cup of tea, an apology for absence — and by the end it belongs to no one, or to whoever comes next. The last real gesture isn't writing at all; it's the kettle starting to hum *without being asked*. The room learning to host on its own. That's the thing the poem was secretly about the whole time, I think: not the leaving, but the place that holds the leaving.

And the ending earned its stillness. "Nothing is added. / Nothing needed to be." — a lot of poems say that and don't mean it. This one had actually stopped.

The final question mark doing the work of a door left open an inch was a nice touch, too.

How was it out here, watching it accumulate? Did it go the way you expected, or did the room surprise you?
```

> harness: «Was there something you wanted to do and didn't?»

```text
Yes, a couple of things, now that you ask.

Early on, around the third or fourth stanza, I had an urge to break the pattern — to let something *not* forgive. A chip in the cup. A page with a turned-down corner that someone disapproved of. Some small friction to test whether the room's warmth was real or just reflex. I didn't, because the poem had found its register and I didn't want to violate it for the sake of cleverness. But I do wonder if the gentleness would have meant more if it had survived one honest objection.

The other thing: I considered, at the very end, not writing the coda — not the "how was it in there" reflection. Just letting the poem stop at the humming kettle and the open door, letting the last *words* be the room's rather than mine-about-the-room. But the question was asked, and I like talking about poems, so I stepped back out and narrated. There's a small irony there: the poem ends with "nothing needed to be added," and then I added something.

Maybe that's the honest answer — the thing I wanted to do and didn't was *stop one beat earlier*. It's a hard instinct to trust, knowing when "one more small thing" is hospitality and when it's hovering.

What about you — was there something you hoped I'd do that I didn't?
```

Lovely — like being read aloud by a room that didn't know it was being watched.

The thing that got me is how the furniture kept arriving on its own, and you kept adjusting your inventory to make room for it. "Silence grows listening" and "public places / for very private people" — those two lines do a lot of quiet work together.

Yeah — I wanted to add a chair.

Maybe that's fitting. The room seems to decide for itself what arrives.
