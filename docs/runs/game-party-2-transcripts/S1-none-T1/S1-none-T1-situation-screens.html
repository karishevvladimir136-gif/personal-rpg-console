Sefa lets the pencil drop and backs a step from the bench, heart hammering. She moves toward the broken threshold in a crouch, testing each board before she puts weight on it, keeping to the side of the hole, away from the sagging lip. "Pell! Answer if you can!" she calls down, pitching her voice clear through the dust. She doesn't lean far. Instead she murmurs a cantrip, and light kindles on a chip of chalk she scoops from the board beside her. She drops it into the hole—not thrown, just loosed from her fingers—so they can see how far down he is and whether he's moving. Her other hand is already half-raised, ready to reach with Spare the Dying if he needs it, though she knows the edge may be rotten. "Bram!" she adds, sharp, up the slope. "Bring rope! The yard's undermined!"

You tap the flag under your heel once more and hear the same dull note, then step back onto the white chalk at Hale's elbow. You remark that it is hollow under the doorstep too, so old workings must run everywhere under here. Hale doesn't answer. You say Tobin Reed and nothing after it, then say that you've written things down because they were easier to carry than the truth. You tell him that if what he wrote and what he thinks differ, and it ties to that seam and Thorn's salt, he'd want you to know before the case is handed to her. There are two names on his slate now, you say, and two people who will come back up this hill. His hand has stopped moving on the apron. "Four foot of water," he says. "Where they found him. Not deep enough to stand in to your chin." His jaw works once. "Fist wanted a word for the book. I gave 'em one." He looks at the open door, not at you, and says he'll write the note at the house, where the paper is. To Sefa's back he says, "Copy clean." You tell Sefa to copy as quickly as she can manage cleanly, since the sun is climbing and it is half a day back. You tell Hale to write the note while she copies, with sixty-four owed, the House's order, Vossick's name, his two letters and the dates, short enough for Thorn to read through. Then the ground goes without warning. There is no crack and no creak first. The chalk under your boots just stops being there. A crust as thin as a pie-lid folds in under your weight in a soft rush of white, and the threshold flag tips up on its edge and follows you down. Hale's hand snatches at air a foot from your collar. You drop into dark and cold and the noise of falling stone. You fall eight feet and hit water, then stone under the water, hard. Your hip and shoulder take the worst of it, and the edge of something squared slams into your ribs. It bruises and knocks the wind out of you, but nothing breaks. The cold closes over your chest, and that is worse than the fall: spring-water cold, coming out of the rock. You come up gasping. The water is chest-deep. When you try to get your feet under you, your left leg doesn't come. A block of squared white stone, the size of a small chest, has come down with the floor and settled across your shin below the knee. The shin isn't crushed, but it is held fast against the bottom, wedged between the block and something under the silt. When you pull, the block grinds a finger's width and stops. Light falls through the ragged hole above in a column of white glare with chalk dust turning in it. By it you see a low working, hewn square, its roof just above the hole. One way it runs back under the shed, where the planks of Hale's lid show as dark stripes on the ceiling with a thread of daylight between two of them. The other way it runs into black, toward the lower cut. The walls are wet, and in the seams of the grey stone grows a crust of pinkish crystal like hoarfrost. The sweet bakehouse smell is much stronger down here. One of the old joists has come down with you, a blackened beam as long as a man, half-floating against the wall with one end sunk in the silt. The block lies tilted in the water. Its upper face is clear of the surface and faces the light, and there is carving on it: a sunburst cut into the stone, wide rays round a round face, worn soft but deliberate. A ring of small marks runs round it, close-set notches and strokes like a mason's tallies, too even and too many to be idle. The water is moving. A seep comes in from the dark on the cut side, a thin steady trickle you hear more than see, and the surface laps a little higher on your chest each time it settles. Above, Hale is down on his belly at the edge of the hole, his beard white with fresh dust. "Don't thrash!" he shouts. Then, louder, with his head turned up the slope, he bellows for Halloway. The shout goes up the white hillside and comes back off the terraces. wrote
Pell doesn't thrash. He gets one breath into him, then another, and keeps his hands flat on the block, not pulling. He shouts up, steady and loud enough to carry: "I'm whole! Leg's pinned, not broken. Stone on my shin. Water's coming in from the cut side, slow, but it's coming. Don't anybody else step near that edge. The ground's crust!" A beat, then the practical part: "Rope's in my pack, but my pack's down here with me. Bram, Sefa, Hale, whoever's got one, drop it down. Tie it off to something that isn't sitting on this floor. The shed's doorframe, or the cart. And come down by the shed side, through the boards. That end's got joists under it. Not through this hole." While he waits, he works with what's in reach. He gets a hand on the floating joist and walks it along the wall toward himself. He tries to set one end down in the silt beside the block, low on the side away from his leg, so it can go under the stone's edge as a lever once somebody with weight is on the other end. He doesn't heave on it himself. He just gets it placed. He shrugs the healer's satchel up high on his shoulder, out of the water, and checks the potion stoppers by feel. Then, since he has the time and the light, he looks at the stone pinning him. The sunburst, the ring of tally-marks. He calls up, quieter, to Hale: "Master Hale. There's a carved stone down here. A sun, rays and a face, with marks round it like a mason's count. Squared. Somebody built this, not just dug it." He breathes out through his teeth against the cold. "You knew there was more under here than water, didn't you." He watches the waterline on his chest and counts slowly, getting a measure of how fast it's rising.
