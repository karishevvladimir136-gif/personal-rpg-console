Pell Dunmore

Claude Fable 5.1
player r5
Osric answers your offer with a short nod and nothing more. To Bram's parting words he says "Always do," and he takes his place in the doorway again, arms folded, to watch you go. Outside, the glare is hard after the cool of the house. Bram wraps the case in his bedroll, checks that the flap of his pack covers it, glances once at the sun and sets off down the track at a road pace. Sefa follows a few steps behind him with the letter at her belt. You let them get ahead, thirty paces or so, and walk with your eyes on the ground. This is your trade, and the dust makes it easy. Below the yard the track runs along the lip of the lower cut, a wide shelf of packed white spoil with the drop on the left. The quarrymen's prints from yesterday go down it, soft-edged and blurred by the night. On top of them are three fresh sets, your own from this morning, coming up. Nothing else. Nobody has come up or gone down since dawn, on foot or on hooves. You are crouched over a crisp heel-print, fairly sure it is Bram's, when the ground under your boots makes a sound like a cracked plate. Then it's gone. The crust of spoil drops out from under you in a single slab, and old timber goes with it. It was a gallery roof, its beams rotted to sponge under a skin of white dust a foot thick. You fall eight feet in a roar of chalk and splinters and land in water. The cold shuts your breath off. Your shoulder hits stone under the surface hard enough to jar your teeth, and something heavy comes down after you: a squared block, shaken loose from the edge of the hole, which slides off the rubble and settles across your left shin. It pins the leg to the gallery floor and doesn't move. It's a bruising fall, a cracked-rib kind of ache down one side and a scraped forearm. Nothing is broken that you can feel. You're lightly hurt, and soaked to the chest. The water is chest-deep, black and cold enough to make your fingers stupid. Daylight comes down through the ragged hole above in a white shaft full of falling dust. By that light you can see a low gallery running off into the dark in both directions. The walls glitter faintly along one side where something rose-white has grown across the stone like frost. Water trickles somewhere you can't see, a thin steady seep. When you look at the stone by your chest, the waterline sits a little higher on it than when you landed. It's slow. Hours slow, not minutes. Then the block on your leg. It's dressed stone, a mason's work and not rubble, about as long as a man is tall and squared on every face. You shove at it with both hands and it doesn't shift a hair, and from down here, with your leg under it, you have nothing to push against. Just under the waterline on the face turned toward you, your fingers find cut lines in the stone. Deliberate curves, a ring of them, and short straight marks around the ring like tallies. In this light you can't make out the shape. Across the gallery lies one of the roof beams that came down with you. It's half sound, old oak, long enough to work as a lever from the lip of the hole. You can see it plainly enough: a rope round the block, the beam under its edge, two people above pulling and prying, and it would roll off your leg in a few minutes. Alone, with no purchase, it's a long hard fight.
