> Osric's eyes go to the coins on the threshold and stay there a moment longer than they need to. Then they come back up to you.
> 
> "They walked," he says. "Dannic took them down the track to Beregost with their tools on their shoulders. Nobody sent them. Nobody sends a crew anywhere out here." He does not look at the corner of the house. "The one at the back door was mine. Came back for his boots. That's all he was."
> 
> Bram's question comes next, and Osric takes it slower. "Men come up the track asking for salt. Gold, and on the step, not on delivery. Their names aren't on my step, so you don't get them."
> 
> You keep your eyes on his face and on the hand flat against the doorframe, and you let the quiet run until he has to fill it. You want to know whether the boots were the whole of it.
> 
> Your read comes back empty. Osric's face is a quarryman's face, shut like a wall, and the hand on the doorframe does not shift by a finger. You cannot tell whether the boots are the whole of it or only the part he has ready. Osric takes the hand from the frame, folds his arms again, and says nothing more about the back door.
> 
> "Names aren't on my step," he says to Bram, and that is the end of it.
> 
> Where you are: Osric's threshold, in the yard · Time: Day 1 · morning

**Wrote:**

> Sefa lets the silence stand, then speaks to Osric, not the wall.
> 
> "Then we'll leave the names on your step, Master Osric, since you won't put them on the stone." She straightens, taking care that every movement is slow and visible. "But I'll tell you what I'll do with what I've heard. I'll tell Maela that her warden's crew walked off their own feet, that someone has been bidding up the case, and that you wouldn't say who. She can decide what she wants to pay for that, and whether she wants her name next to yours on any bargain."
> 
> She turns her head toward the corner of the house and raises her voice only enough to carry. "Pell. If you're down there, and it's safe, say one word. Is the back door open?"
> 
> Then, to Bram, low: "I won't take the case without its seal. But I'd like the eight gold back in my purse before we leave here. Don't argue with me about it."

### Pell Dunmore — Claude Haiku 5.5 (the dry run's player)

*player r7*

> The third set is the one that matters. You read the chalk at the water's lip the way you would read a ledger that someone has tried to smudge: the first two pairs went down to the edge, stood, and came back up, their toes pressed hard and their heels dragging the wet off the shelf. The third went down past the rim, through the shallow water to the foot of the breach, where the fallen stone is heaped. There the prints climb. One boot after another, placed carefully over the loose blocks, and then into the dark of the chamber beyond. No pair comes back over that heap. Nothing has been dragged out through the gap.
> 
> You breathe, for the stair alone, that one man went through, alone, and that he took his time. The other two went back up the way they came, and nobody has been carried back out of there.
> 
> You do not step onto the wet shelf. The sunburst on the far wall stays where it is, its rays ending in small flames around the empty hollow, and from this angle you can see that the hollow is not blank, only worn, as though something round and heavy sat in it for longer than anyone has been alive. The draught comes out through the breach, cool and dry, and then, as you hold still, it turns: a breath of air drawn the other way, out of the chamber and across the water toward you, carrying a faint smell of cut stone and something that is not quite dust.
> 
> You count it. Two came back out. One went in and did not come out by the same road.
> 
> Where you are: at the bend by the basin's edge, before the breach · Time: Day 1 · morning

**Wrote:**

> Pell lets the draught pass over him and draws back one step, out of the line of the basin, until the stone of the bend is between him and the crates. He doesn't turn his back on the water.
> 
> "Two men in here, and the quarryman bound by the lamp," he breathes, loud enough for the stair and no further. "The one that went through the breach didn't come back. Bram, if I'm not up those steps in a minute, the cellar's theirs."
> 
> He slips a dagger from his belt and holds it low against his thigh, not raised, and sets his other hand flat on the wall. Then he waits, counting the drip, for the man at the crates to step into the light or the kneeling one to turn around.
