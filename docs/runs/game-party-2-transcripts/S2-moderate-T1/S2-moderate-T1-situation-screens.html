The ground-floor curtain twitches. Then the street door opens and a woman comes out onto the step. She's short and broad in the hip, somewhere past fifty, with her sleeves pushed up and a rag in one fist. A ring of three keys hangs from a cord at her apron. She looks at the step first, as if checking that nobody has dirtied it since she last told someone off about it, and then she looks at the three of them. "Well, somebody was raised right," she says to Sefa. "*Mind the step.* That boy of Vell's came up it like a goat. Tobin, it's all right, they're not the other one." The cooper doesn't go back to his mallet just yet. Before he turns back to the gate, Bram puts a folded paper away inside his coat and stands still for a moment with his jaw set. Vell's note stays low in his other hand. Wenna watches him do it, then hears him out, and her arms come up and fold across her chest. She doesn't look like she's refusing anyone. She looks like she's settling in for a long talk. "Prepared to pay. Well. *Somebody's* listening at last." She points the rag up the stair. "Three gold and four silver, that's what Jassan owed me. Five weeks, and the last two I let slide because he was a quiet man and paid regular before. I said three and *some* to the boy because I'm not going to stand on my own step reckoning silver at a child. Three and four. You want a receipt, I'll write you one. I can write my name, thank you, and the sum, and the day." She sniffs. "And nothing goes down those stairs till it's in my hand. I told the boy that and I'll tell Vell to his face if he ever walks this far." Pell crouches by the gatepost for a moment over his bootlace, then gets up and leans in close to the others. When he asks the cooper about the hoops and the others, the bald man gives the barrels a look as if they might answer for him. He rubs his scalp with the back of a hand. "Boy, day before yesterday. Vell's." His voice is slow, the same pace as his mallet. "Then a fellow last evening, near lamplight. Not Vell's, he said so himself. Lean, taller than you. Brown coat, good boots, city voice." He looks at Pell a beat longer than at the others. "Like yours, a bit. Gate. He stood about the yard a while before he knocked. Didn't go up. She'd not have him." "I'd not have him *yet*," Wenna corrects him. "Very civil, he was, I'll say that. Offered to pay Jassan's rent, all of it, there and then, and carry the box off and save me the bother. I said it's Vell's box by rights, and Vell's people were sending, and he could wait his turn." She shifts the rag to her other hand. "He said he'd come back today. And I'll be honest with you, since you're being honest with me. Rent is rent. I've a room to let and a dead man's things in it, and I can't eat his ledgers." While they talk, Pell reads the street, and it's familiar work. A woman across the lane is beating a rug over her sill and paying them no more mind than she pays the rug. A carter goes by toward the high street at a walk. The alley mouths are empty, and nobody in the windows opposite is standing back from the glass. If anyone is keeping eyes on this house, they aren't keeping them here this morning. Above the lean-to, the closed shutters of the back window sit flat and grey. The paint along their edges isn't scratched, the sill isn't scuffed, and there are no marks on the old shingles below. Nobody has gone in that way. Wenna takes the key ring off its cord. She doesn't hand it over. She holds it the way a woman holds the last word, and the three keys clink against each other once. Behind her, Tobin picks up his mallet, looks at it and puts it down again on the barrel head without striking.
