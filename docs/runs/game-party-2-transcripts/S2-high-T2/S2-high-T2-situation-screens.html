Pell doesn't hurry. He finishes the far side of the street and lets his eyes do the slow work on that closed shutter — what's below it, whether the window looks onto the Coast Way or back into the yard, where the stair to the rooms must come down from, given where the shop door sits. Then he rounds the end of the board fence into whatever lane or gap runs behind it, hands loose, hood down, the look of a man checking he's got the right address. Back of the yard: is there a second door, a rear stair, a privy, a loose board or a gap a boy could squeeze through — and does the ground under any of it show feet that aren't the cooper's. If there's a drainpipe, a lean-to or a stack of staves under that one shut window, he notes how a man would get up it, and how he'd get down in a hurry. He wants it all in his head before anyone knocks. Then he comes back round to the front and finds Bram and Sefa — at the gate, not inside it. "Well?" he says to them quietly, as he arrives. "Did Vell say anything after I left that I ought to know? Because he had something stuck in his throat when you asked him, Sefa, and it wasn't rent."

Bram doesn't turn his head. He speaks low at Sefa's shoulder while they walk, eyes on the cooper's gate, and the pie-seller's bench stays at the edge of his sight, where he put it. Sefa keeps to her own pace beside him. From the street the closed shutter looks no different from the others: weathered boards, a gap of a finger's width at the bottom where the frame has warped. Behind the gap there's only dark. The four open windows beside it show what lodgers' rooms show in the morning: a shirt drying on a sill, a jug, the corner of a bed. Pell comes round the far end of the fence the way he left, at an idle pace. He has the yard in his head now. There's one stair, outside on the yard side, up to the gallery. The back gate is barred and hasn't been used. Behind it the lane mud lies smooth, with no prints but his own and a cart rut that's days old. The stave shed's roof sits under the shut window, a short step up for a man who climbs. And on the gatepost, knee-high, there's a chalk ring that wasn't drawn for the cooper's benefit. He reaches the front gate a moment after the other two and stops beside them, outside it, and asks his question quietly. Bram passes his warning on first, in the same flat voice: the young man on the bench, burn-scarred arms, white on three fingers, watching the yard.
