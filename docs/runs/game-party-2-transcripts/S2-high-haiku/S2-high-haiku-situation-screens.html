Beside you, Sefa stepped close to Wenna with no weapon in reach and paid the rent, three gold and four silver, from her own purse, saying Master Vell would settle with her later. She kept the coins held out so Wenna had to look at her, asked after Wenna's back and the child on the step, and asked for the key and the cord with it. Then she asked who else had climbed the stairs since the Watch came, anyone who came asking, came to take something, or was turned away. Wenna's hand closes over the coins Sefa holds out. She counts them without looking down, the way a woman counts who has taken rent for years. Your hand is still open with three silver pieces in it. She glances at them and shakes her head. "One payment's enough. I've had mine." You tip the silver back into your pouch. "So." She tucks the coins into her apron pocket. "My back's the same, thank you. Tam's my grandson, and he'll be a thief if nobody stops him, so you're right about the quick." She looks at Sefa a moment longer than she needs to, then holds up the key and the cord it hangs from. "Jassan's. The key's for the room door. The little brass one on the cord is for his box, I'd think. He never let it off his neck." She puts both into Sefa's palm and does not let go of the cord until Sefa has her fingers round it. "The Watch boy came first, the morning I found him. The one with the cold in his nose. He went up, wrote in a book on the landing, came down and sneezed into my yard. He took nothing out that I saw, and I stood at the foot of the stair the whole time." She folds her arms. "Then the physician from the Song of the Morning, the second day. Looked, said it was what you'd expect with a man of his build, and went down." She shifts her weight, and her eyes go past you to the yard gate. "And a quiet man in a grey coat came round twice. From a counting-house on the Trade Way, he said, though he didn't say which. He wanted to know what was in the room, and he had an open purse and offered me more than the rent for it. I told him to ask the Watch." Her mouth tightens. "He didn't care for that." To you, flatly: "The desk? I never touched his papers. I went up the once, to see him, and came straight down. Whoever put that stool square, it wasn't me." She looks at you as if you should be the one to explain it. "Door's been shut since. I've had the key in my apron. Nobody's gone in but the Watch, and they went in with my boy holding the lamp." She steps back from the step and lets the boy on it slide past her into the house, then looks at Sefa again. "Up you go. The door sticks at the top. Lift it, don't shove it." At the last, dryly, as she turns to her yard: "And Master Vell can settle his own accounts with you. I've had mine." wrote
I fold Odda Brand's letter small and push it into my belt pouch, under the coins. Neither of the others sees it, and I keep my face where it was. Sefa has the key and the brass cord. I put a hand on the door frame and say, low enough that Wenna won't hear over the yard, "Up we go, and I go first. Keep your voice down." The stairs are steep and narrow, with a turn at the top. I take them one step at a time and check each tread for scuffs, a smear of wet, anything that doesn't look like a cooper's dust. I've walked enough wagon ramps to read the wood. At the landing I stop and look back at the door. Wenna's voice drifts up from the yard, pitched to carry, telling the boy to fetch the water. "Sefa," I say. "Before you put that key in, tell me what you make of the quiet man in the grey coat. Counting-house on the Trade Way, open purse, and Wenna sent him off to the Watch. A man who wants the room that badly doesn't go away after one refusal. And somebody tidied that desk before Vell sent for us. I'd like to know who else has been standing in that room."

Sefa tucks the cord into her palm, the little brass key warm from Wenna's hand, and nods to the old woman as if they had agreed on everything. "Thank you, Mistress Pike. Your back is in good hands with the boy, I think." She turns to Bram. "Come up with me. Stand at the top of the stairs, where you can see the landing and the street door both. If anyone climbs after us, I want to hear it before they reach the door." At the top she lifts the door as Wenna told her, and it comes free without a sound. The room is cold and smells of lamp oil and damp wool. A narrow bed is made up square. A table holds an empty cup, a pen, and a stack of paper weighted with a stone. The shutter on the back wall is closed, and a grey thread of light comes around its edge. Before she goes in, Sefa stops on the landing where the Watch clerk's book lies open on a shelf by the door. She runs one finger down the page without touching the ink, reading the cramped hand. "'Third day of the month, morning. Jassan Ferr, clerk, found dead in bed. No wound. Face dark, as if from strangling or a fit. Door locked from within. Key with the landlady. Nothing taken that is known.'" She frowns. "Then a second line, added after, in a different ink: 'Strongbox noted, not opened, not removed.' There's no name under it. Only the Watch's stamp, and the stamp is smudged." She straightens and looks at Bram. "Nothing taken that is known. Someone wrote that line knowing it might be untrue. Keep your eyes on that door." Then she turns to the room, and her gaze goes straight to the floor by the bed, where the boards are a shade paler in a square the size of a strongbox.

You cross the wall as if the lane were empty, going along the coping and down onto the slate of the wash-house roof without a tile shifting. The watcher you were looking for is where the chalk said he would be: a man in a brown jerkin sitting on an upturned cart at the lane's end, facing the house's front door, eating an apple and not looking up. Nobody in the lane turns their head. The shutter on the back wall is a plank frame with no glass, latched on the inside by a bar that drops into an iron keeper. You set your fingers on the sill and look at the keeper before you touch anything. The bar sits square, but the wood round the keeper is pale and fresh, with a thin bright scrape where something narrow has been worked under the bar and lifted. The scrape is not three days old. Dust from the slates has settled over the rest of the sill, and it has not settled over that. Below you the yard is quiet. The barrels creak once in the wind and are still. Pell lies still on the slate for a breath, then reaches for the sill without letting his weight shift the tiles. "Someone's been in," he says under his breath, and it is not a question. He thumbs the pale scrape, then sights along the bar from the side, mouth tight. "Thin blade, worked under and lifted. Not a Watch man's crowbar. Someone who knew how to open a shutter without leaving a mess." He leans closer, eyes on the iron keeper. "Lifted once, set back. Sloppy with the wood, neat with the bar. Could be the same hand that sent the watcher to the cart, or just somebody who came before us and didn't want the landlady to know." He draws his dagger slowly, not to use it, just to hold the point of it under the bar and test the lift without moving the latch. "I'm not going through the shutter yet. The bar's been set, so whoever lifted it set it for a reason. I'll see if it moves for me the same way before I put my weight on it." He glances down at the yard, then toward the street where Sefa's voice would be, though he can't hear it from here. "Sefa, Wenna's door. I've got a window that's been opened by someone who isn't Wenna. Tell me what she says about the stairs."
the DM's narration of round 4 — Bram Halloway, Sefa Marrin (never shown to a player as it is; each screen is the relay's retelling)
Bram sets his back to the wall at the top of the stairs and turns the shield so its rim clears the frame. Below, Wenna tells the boy to fetch water, and the yard gate creaks once and settles. Nobody climbs. Sefa goes in. The room is as Wenna described it: cold, the bed made square, the shutter closed with its thread of grey light. Beside the bed the floorboards carry a clean rectangle, sharp-edged and a shade paler than the boards around it. Dust lies thin over everything else in the room and does not lie in that shape. Something the size of a strongbox stood there, and was lifted. The strongbox is not on the mark. It is pushed under the far side of the bed frame, its oak corner and one iron band showing in the gap, squared to the wall as neatly as the stool was pushed under the desk at Vell's. On the corner of the lid nearest the light there is a fleck of dark green wax, and in it the edge of a stamp. Sefa can make out the scales. Above them, or beside them, something else is pressed into the wax, and she cannot see it yet from where she stands. Bram slides the folded letter into his belt pouch, under the coins, and closes the flap before anyone below can see his hands. He takes the stairs one tread at a time, as he said he would. The wood is dry and dusted with cooper's sawdust, with nothing on it that was not there before, and at the landing he turns to look back at the street door while Wenna's voice carries up from the yard. His question to Sefa stands in the room, unanswered. Sefa has not yet given her reply, and that reply is hers to give. No roll was needed for the letter or the stairs. Bram's question waits for Sefa's player.
the DM's narration of round 4 — Pell Dunmore (never shown to a player as it is; each screen is the relay's retelling)
The bar gives under the point of the dagger. Pell works the blade in beneath the iron, feels for the lip of the keeper, and lifts. The bar rises a finger's width and stops. The shutter does not stir, and the only sound is a breath of old wood. Whoever did this before him used the same angle and set the bar back in the same place, so the keeper is worn bright along one side. Pell eases the bar down into the iron again, slowly, until it sits. The pale scrape stays as it was. Through the gap between plank and frame he can see a slice of the room: a strip of cold boards, the foot of a narrow bed, and at the edge of the light the corner of an oak box pushed under the frame, one iron band catching the grey. Nothing moves in there. Below in the yard the barrels hold still. In the lane the watcher's cart creaks as the man shifts his weight, and he does not look up from his apple. Pell stays flat on the slate and waits for the lane to settle before he does anything else.
