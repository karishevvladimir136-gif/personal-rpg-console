```markdown
## Recommendation: fix the test apparatus, then test whether tighter pacing preserves what works

I would **not buy another broad round of fresh openings this week**. These sessions already identify several reproducible defects. More openings would mostly rediscover them while giving unreliable comparisons between players and campaign briefs.

Authorize **up to $50**, in stages, and leave **$10 uncommitted**. These are spending ceilings, not estimates of session prices; use actual metered costs before launching each stage.

### What the evidence supports

**The fiction has a strong foundation.** Across the notes, players repeatedly value distinct NPCs, sensory detail, the familiar, meaningful refusals, and mysteries emerging from ordinary life. Preserve those features.

**The apparatus is materially affecting play.** Eight of 30 sessions—27%—ended under the harness’s stuck rule. That includes five of six DeepSeek sessions and three of six Opus sessions. This is evidence of an interaction problem, not a reliable ranking of their ability to play. Wrong modifiers, misdirected rerolls, and stale logs also changed choices: one player explicitly avoided another card operation because the machinery had become untrustworthy.

**Pacing deserves a controlled test, not a wholesale rewrite.** Several players wanted less routine accounting and earlier actionable discoveries. Others loved the patient opening. The useful question is whether we can remove repetition and interface overhead without losing atmosphere.

Finally, **no player voluntarily ending does not establish retention or satisfaction**. All sessions were censored by a cap or a technical stop. The positive notes are useful qualitative evidence from AI players, not a substitute for human playtesting.
```

```markdown
**Yes—but it changes the closeout, not the case for more spending.** I would now recommend **$0 on additional play or interviews**. Keep the conditional repair-verification allowance only if the app will actually be used again.

The reader’s concerns are valid in two distinct ways:

- **The second question encourages a particular kind of answer.** It invites players to retrieve—or construct—a moment of inhibited action. Those answers cannot establish how common frustration was, or that players spontaneously regarded restraint as a problem. They remain useful examples to investigate, especially where the transcript corroborates them.
- **The notes have an audience incentive.** Writing to the owner may encourage praise, tactful criticism, and claims of wanting to return. The invitation to describe “what broke” does solicit criticism, but it does not remove that pressure. With AI players, these are generated evaluations, not independent measurements of enjoyment.

Neither concern makes the feedback worthless. It changes the weight it should carry:

| Evidence | Appropriate use |
|---|---|
| Visible wrong arithmetic, swallowed messages, incorrect reroll targets | Direct evidence of defects |
| Reports that routine exchanges dragged | Qualitative product feedback, with possible audience effects |
| “I wanted to act but didn’t” | Candidate explanations, checked against the transcript |
| “I’d come back” or elaborate descriptions of temptation | Not evidence of retention or an independently measured internal experience |

In particular, **do not respond by making temptation stronger simply because the follow-up elicited requests for it**. Some answers describe successful character restraint; others describe blocked controls or lost opportunities. They are not one outcome.

### Revised closeout

Before treating the reports as final, make a **brief methods-and-limitations correction**, using the existing material:

1. State the exact prompts and that feedback was addressed to the owner.
2. Distinguish spontaneous comments in the first note from answers elicited by the second question.
3. Qualify any claims about enjoyment, agency, demand for stronger temptation, or willingness to return.
4. Keep transcript-confirmed defects separate from players’ interpretations of their effects.

If a central conclusion rests on those subjective reports, revise that conclusion—not just a footnote. Otherwise, a short addendum is enough. There is no need to reopen the study or spend money trying to obtain “unbiased” answers from players who have already been prompted.

**My recommendation remains: close the research, preserve the money, and verify repairs only for a concrete future use.** The new information strengthens the need for careful reporting; it does not create a reason to buy more sessions.
```
