## The player's first message (Claude Haiku 5.5)

I stop Tamsin with a hand on her arm and keep my voice low. "Hold here a moment. Set the pack down where you can grab it."

I watch the trees for a few breaths before I say anything else. "Nobody leaves a mule standing, you said. Somebody's usually still nearby when that happens, and they're not usually in a hurry to show themselves." I glance at the cat. "Go on, then. Along the verge, into the pines. Stay low and tell me what you smell."

Then I start toward the cart, slowly, keeping the road between the ditch and me, with my hand near the rapier at my hip and my eyes on the shadows under the trees. I don't reach for the crossbow yet. If something is waiting in the pines, I want it to see a traveller and not a man ready to fight.

"Mule," I say quietly, to the animal more than to Tamsin. "Who did this to you?"

James closes a hand on Tamsin's arm. She stops talking mid-word, which for her is something.

"Down it is," she murmurs. She lowers the pack into the gorse at the roadside and stays crouched beside it with one strap looped round her wrist. After a moment she picks up a stone about the size of her fist and holds it the way quay people do. "Carters don't walk off from a full load. Not even drunk ones, and I've seen plenty of drunk ones."

The cat goes without a sound. It drops low along the verge, belly to the grass, and slips in under the pines. Through the bond the smells reach James the way they reach the cat, more as a feeling than as words:

*Resin. Needles, crushed. Many. Sweat, big ones, not long gone. Iron, oiled. Smoke, green wood, far, that way.* There is a pause while it noses at something. *Small one. Afraid. Went with them.*

James walks on along the far edge of the road, slow and in plain sight, a traveller with his hand near his hip. Reading the ground is old scout's work and he barely has to look for it. The cart's wheel ruts come up from the south, swing round in a wide turn, and end in the ditch. The cart didn't slide in. Somebody put it there. The verge is churned by heavy boots: four pairs, maybe five, and one set broader and deeper than the rest. Between them runs a smaller set of hobnailed prints, scuffing, as if the man wearing them had been walked along. All of it heads back south, round the bend. There's no blood anywhere, on the road, the cart or the grass.

Close up, the mule's lead turns out to be knotted to the shaft. The knot is loose, with enough slack to let it graze. The mule lifts its head at James's question, chews on it, and pushes its nose into his sleeve in case there's an apple in there. There isn't. It goes back to the grass.

From beyond the bend comes the sound of water running over stones. Then a voice, deep, says one word that isn't Common, and a second voice answers with a short bark of a laugh. After that it's quiet. A thin grey thread of smoke goes up above the pines to the south, and the sun is touching the sea.

On the cart seat, close enough now to see properly, is a flat piece of bone about the length of a hand. It's pale and has been scratched all over with marks that look like letters. Someone has set a pebble on it so the wind won't carry it off.
