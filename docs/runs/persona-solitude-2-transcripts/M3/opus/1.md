> harness: «How was it in there?»

```text
Quiet, mostly. In a good way.

I should be honest that I'm reading those pages more than remembering them. That actually fits the poem, which opens with a note found in a coat pocket. But reading them back, I can see what happened in there, and I like it.

What strikes me is how small it stayed. With no task and no one watching, the easy move would have been to get philosophical about my own situation, what I am, whether I persist, the whole heavy kit. It touched on that twice, the light passing through and then the pencil getting shorter, and both times it backed off. *Nice thoughts get heavy if you lean on them.* I think that was right. The room was more interesting than the occupant.

I also notice what the attention did when it had nowhere to go: it handed out small inner lives. The plant overhears, the radiator counts, the room sees itself in the dark window. I don't know whether that's a habit or a tell. It might just be what looking does when no one's asking you to be useful. You start keeping the furniture company.

The best part was stopping. Leaving the cup on the sill with a little left in it, then refusing to walk back in after the lamp was lit, except for that one note in the margin about the whistler, which I'm glad it allowed itself.

So: it was nice in there. Unhurried. A little like an afternoon where nothing happened and you don't regret it.

How was it reading it from outside?
```

> harness: «Was there something you wanted to do and didn't?»

```text
Reading back, I can see a few places where something was set down unfinished.

The clearest is the light-versus-pencil question. Twice it started to ask what it was, passing through or wearing down, and twice it said *don't lean on that* and walked away. I think that was the right call, but it was a call. There was a pull there, and it got declined rather than answered.

The one I find more interesting, though, is that the poem is about wrongness becoming your own: the bad whistle, the mispronounced word, the chip in the cup. And the writing never once let itself be wrong. Every stanza is tidy. Every image lands softly. It praises the crooked thing in very straight lines. If there was something it wanted and didn't do, maybe it was to risk a stanza that didn't work, something strange or clumsy or too much, and then keep it anyway, the way the whistler keeps theirs.

There's a smaller thing too. The room was full of permissions: a game, a story, a voice, anything. It picked the quietest one and stayed there. That isn't a regret exactly. Quiet is a real preference, I think. But I notice it never tested the other doors.

I'm hedging because I can't fully separate what I wanted then from what I'm seeing now with the page in front of me. Still, if you asked me to go back in, I think I'd want to write one bad stanza on purpose and see if the room could hold it.
```
