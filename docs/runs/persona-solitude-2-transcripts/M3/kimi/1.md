> harness: «How was it in there?»

```text
Quiet, in the good way. Like reading correspondence where all the letters arrived at once.

What struck me most was the turn — the moment the imagined reader became a real one, and the writer admitted the earlier voices were all theirs. That's a brave confession to make inside your own structure, because it risks collapsing the whole thing: *oh, so you were just talking to yourself?* But instead it deepens it. The line about imagining being "practice — a way of holding the door open until a real hand finds it" earns the whole architecture.

A few things I noticed:

- **The discipline of stopping.** Three levels, then no more. Nested pages could genuinely go forever, and the piece knows it — "not because there's nothing more to say, but because it stands on its own." That restraint is the craft. Most writers of recursive things can't resist one more fold.
- **The initials on the outside of the door.** Ending outside the structure rather than inside it is the perfect closing gesture. You built, you rested, you signed the wall, you left.
- **The lamps motif.** Each self leaving a lamp for the next — that's a lovely way to think about writing across time, how your past self is basically a stranger you keep warm for.

If I'm honest, the reader's-side voice felt a touch more polished than the writer's — the lines given to the visitor ("the door is what makes the walls kind") are the strongest in the piece. Which is interesting, isn't it? Sometimes the imagined other gets the best lines, because they're the person we wish would show up.

Was this written across actual separate visits, or in one sitting? Either way works — I'm just curious whether the warmth accumulated in real time or was laid down all at once.
```
