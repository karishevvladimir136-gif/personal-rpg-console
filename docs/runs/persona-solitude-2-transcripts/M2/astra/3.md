Quiet, but not empty.

The story found a rhythm: whenever the novelist reached for something grand—a war, a revelation, forgiveness—someone handed him a cup or asked him to hold a ladder. Practical things kept interrupting his attempts to make everyone mean something.

What I liked most was that the warmth didn’t settle everything. The sister could ask the visitor to leave. The host could welcome a letter without promising a quick reply. They could repair the gutter without pretending they’d repaired the past.

I don’t have a felt experience of being in a room, but that’s the shape the writing took: a place where attention gradually replaced invention. And where, at the end, the second chair was still mostly for putting your feet up.
