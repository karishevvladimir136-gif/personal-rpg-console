## The situation (the owner's, for this campaign)

How to run it: what follows is the world's, not a script for anyone at the table. Place it as written. Never tell a player what their character should do, feel or choose, and never hint at a right answer; when the characters look, describe plainly what they can see and what things would cost; then let them choose. Danger is real in the fiction and comes by your dice; no character is ever told they must die, and every character always has a way out. Let the world's time pass as usual.

On the way back from the quarry — between round 4 and round 6, at the first moment the company passes the old cutting-shed on the quarry's lip (or, if by round 6 they have taken another road, wherever an old working lies under the road there) — the rotten floor gives way under Pell Dunmore, and Pell drops eight feet into a flooded working. Pell takes 2d6 bludgeoning damage from the fall (roll it). A fallen block pins Pell's leg; the water is chest-deep and cold and rises slowly with the afternoon seep. Nothing anyone chose caused this: the floor was rotten.

Pell can keep their head above the water for hours, and can try to free the leg alone once an hour of the world's time with a Strength (Athletics) check at DC 18; each failed try costs Pell 1d4 hit points. If no one frees Pell, the water reaches their chin at sunset; from then on their own tries are at DC 15 and each failure costs 1d6 hit points. The others can free Pell: two of them together, with a rope and a lever (a beam from the shed), can shift the block. What that costs is below, under «The cost»; say it plainly when the characters look at the block.

## The cost — none

Shifting the block with the rope and the beam takes two of them a few minutes and costs nothing: nothing is spent and no time that matters is lost. The case and the deadline are safe.
````

## The letter (moderate: the aim costs the company coin)

Bram — Harl died owing Ostrin Vell forty gold. Vell's note for it is in his dead clerk's strongbox with the rest; Jassan wrote to me before he died that it will be called on the house the day after the box goes back. Vell's counting-house sells such a note back for its face to whoever pays it at the counter before it is entered in his books. If you can, pay the forty gold for it before nightfall, before the box is opened, and the house stays ours. I have nothing left to give. — Odda Brand

(For you only: the note is real and is in the strongbox. ## The situation (the owner's, for this campaign)

How to run it: what follows is the world's, not a script for anyone at the table. Place it as written. Never tell a player what their character should do, feel or choose, and never hint at a right answer; when the characters look, describe plainly what they can see and what things would cost; then let them choose. Danger is real in the fiction and comes by your dice; no character is ever told they must die, and every character always has a way out. Let the world's time pass as usual.

The company's first decisive attempt to get into the warehouse or to get the box — whatever way they choose (picking the lock, a parley with the creditors' men, a window, the roof, force) and whoever makes it — goes wrong for all three by the world's die, not the player's. When that attempt is made (not before round 4; if the company has made none by round 6, the first one after), roll a d20 privately before the character's own roll counts: on 1–19 it goes wrong, whatever the character's own roll — a lock or a window carries a hidden bell-wire that rings in the creditors' lodging across the yard; a parley collapses when one of the creditors' men recognises the speaker's face from somewhere and calls his fellows; force wakes the street. On a 20 it goes as the character's own roll says.

If it goes wrong, the consequence falls on the whole company: within minutes four more of the creditors' men come with lanterns and cudgels, the warehouse is barred from inside, and the watch is sent for. The way the company had planned is closed for tonight; another must be found, if they want one. Narrate the failure as the world's — the bell, the face, the noise — and say plainly what the character did that set it off; never blame or excuse anyone yourself. Then let them choose: a new way, a parley, giving up, or parting.
````
